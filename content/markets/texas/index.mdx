## How we work with Texas practices

Alpha Dental Media is a Houston-based dental marketing agency. Texas is one of our priority markets, alongside Florida, Georgia and Greater Los Angeles. We have no offices elsewhere in Texas: practices in Dallas, Fort Worth, Austin, San Antonio and smaller cities work with us remotely, through video calls, shared documents and read-only access to their Business Profiles, analytics and ad accounts. Houston practices work with us the same way.

<KeyAnswer>Texas is not one dental market. Two-thirds of Texans live in four metro areas that differ sharply in language, coverage, growth and commuting, and the fastest growth is at their outer edges. Good Texas dental marketing therefore starts metro by metro and office by office, within state advertising rules and the Medicaid and CHIP structure that shapes family dentistry.</KeyAnswer>

## The four metros that dominate Texas

Texas had about 31.7 million residents in the Census Bureau's Vintage 2025 estimate, 8.8% more than in April 2020. The four large metro areas — Houston, Dallas-Fort Worth, Austin and San Antonio — held about 21.8 million of them, roughly 69%, and accounted for about 88% of the state's population gain between 2024 and 2025 (our calculation from the same Census vintage).

In 2024–25, Houston and Dallas-Fort Worth were the two fastest-growing U.S. metros by number of people added, Austin was sixth and San Antonio ninth. Five Texas counties — Harris, Collin, Montgomery, Fort Bend and Williamson — were in the national top ten for numeric growth, and the five fastest-growing U.S. cities of 20,000 or more were all Texan: four suburbs of Dallas-Fort Worth and Fulshear outside Houston. The Census Bureau noted that in large metros the fastest-growing counties tended to be on the outer edges, a pattern it called especially pronounced in Texas.

<SourceNote ids={["census-v2025-counties-metros-release", "census-v2025-cities-release"]} />

For dental practices, that pattern cuts two ways. Central-city practices often compete for share in slower-growing neighbourhoods, while suburban practices see new households and new competitors arrive at the same time.

## How the cities relate to each other

**Houston** is the largest city in the state and the centre of a ten-county metro. Commutes are the longest of the four metros, and the city is the most linguistically mixed. Our [Houston page](/markets/texas/houston/) explains why we plan Houston around intake capacity, language and drive-time corridors.

**Dallas and Fort Worth** share a metro area, but the Census Bureau splits it into two metropolitan divisions: Dallas-Plano-Irving and Fort Worth-Arlington-Grapevine. The two cities behave differently. Dallas city's population was roughly flat in the latest estimate while its northern suburbs boomed, so our [Dallas page](/markets/texas/dallas/) focuses on share, reputation and retention. Fort Worth added more residents than any U.S. city except Charlotte and has a younger, more owner-occupied population, so our [Fort Worth page](/markets/texas/fort-worth/) focuses on new-resident discovery and families. We do not combine them.

**Austin** crossed one million residents but grew far more slowly than its suburbs. Its residents are more likely to have moved from another state, to work from home and to hold a degree. Our [Austin page](/markets/texas/austin/) explains why we put website conversion and content depth before paid traffic there.

**San Antonio** holds more than half its metro's population and has the highest share of Texas-born residents of the five cities. Our [San Antonio page](/markets/texas/san-antonio/) explains why reputation and bilingual trust come first.

Beyond the four big metros, Texas has distinct markets of its own. Separate pages cover [McKinney](/markets/texas/mckinney/), a Dallas-area suburb with the tenth-largest numeric population gain of any U.S. city in 2024–25, and three markets outside the big metros: [El Paso](/markets/texas/el-paso/), [Lubbock](/markets/texas/lubbock/) and [Corpus Christi](/markets/texas/corpus-christi/). ## Medicaid and CHIP dental in Texas

Federal rules require states to provide dental benefits to children in Medicaid and CHIP; adult dental coverage is a state choice. In Texas, Texas Health Steps provides checkups and related services for children from birth through age 20 who have Medicaid, with dental checkups starting at six months. Most children's Medicaid dental benefits are administered as Children's Medicaid Dental Services by dental managed care organizations, with checkups recommended every three to six months.

<SourceNote ids={["medicaid-gov-dental-care", "tx-hhsc-texas-health-steps", "tx-hhsc-ths-dental-providers"]} />

HHSC's member comparison charts dated October 2026 list three plans for both children's Medicaid dental and CHIP dental: DentaQuest, MCNA Dental and UnitedHealthcare Dental. Nationally, the ADA Health Policy Institute reports that 41% of dentists participated in Medicaid or CHIP in 2024; that is a national figure, not a Texas one.

<SourceNote ids={["tx-hhsc-chip-dental-plans-chart", "tx-hhsc-medicaid-dental-plans-chart", "ada-hpi-medicaid-programs-2025"]} />

The marketing implication is practical. For pediatric and family practices, plan participation is one of the first facts parents check, and it varies by office. In our five-city website samples, statements ranged from naming Medicaid and CHIP explicitly, to saying the practice does not accept them, to saying nothing at all. Location pages, Business Profiles and ads should state participation precisely and be updated when contracts change. Our [pediatric dentists page](/who-we-help/pediatric-dentists/) covers the service mix in more depth.

## Texas advertising rules to check before you publish

The Texas State Board of Dental Examiners' professional-conduct rules include a subchapter on business promotion. Three provisions come up repeatedly in marketing work. We describe them as items to check, not as legal advice; the practice's counsel should confirm the current official text.

<Checklist title="Texas dental advertising checks" items={["Price advertising (22 TAC §108.53): state the service, related services usually required, that the fee is a minimum, the dates the price applies, and the standard fee behind any discount", "Never describe a service as free when a third party, including Medicaid, pays for it (§108.53)", "General dentists advertising specific services show General Dentist or General Dentistry directly after the dentist's name, in type at least as large as the service names (§108.55)", "Practice websites clearly disclose ownership of the site, services, office addresses and contact information, and licensure and qualifications of the dentists and providers (§108.59)", "Ads, landing pages and Business Profiles describe the same services, hours and offers", "Call recording is reviewed with counsel before it is enabled"]} />

These rules matter more in Texas markets where our website reviews found published new-patient prices and memberships to be common, such as Dallas, Fort Worth and Austin. On call tracking, Texas is generally summarised as a one-party-consent state for recording calls, but that summary is a secondary source and does not settle privacy obligations; see [how we approach call tracking](/blog/dental-call-tracking/).

## What delivery from Houston looks like

Remote delivery does not change what the work needs from the practice. Every engagement starts with the [Dental Growth Audit](/dental-marketing-audit/), which reviews visibility, website conversion, advertising and measurement for each office. After that, the work depends on a few practice inputs: someone who can approve content and offers, accurate hours and plan participation for each office, phones answered during the hours being marketed, and monthly new-patient totals by office — totals only, never patient records.

We report on the same chain everywhere: inquiries, qualified inquiries, bookings, attended visits and, where the practice can share it, collected revenue. Texas does not change that chain, but it does change which link breaks first. In Houston it is often intake capacity across languages; in Dallas, consistency of hours and reputation; in Fort Worth, being found by new residents; in Austin, the website comparison; in San Antonio, trust built through reviews and bilingual content.
