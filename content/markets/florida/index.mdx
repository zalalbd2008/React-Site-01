## How we work with Florida practices from Houston

We are based in Houston and do not have a Florida office. Every service we offer for Florida practices — search visibility, Google Business Profile work, Google Ads, websites, reviews and measurement — is delivered remotely, with the practice's own accounts and data.

Remote delivery works when the practice supplies what a remote team cannot observe. For a Florida practice, that means:

- **Real operating information:** hours by day, any Saturday or evening sessions, and who answers the phone at lunch.
- **Languages the team can actually serve:** which staff can take a booking call in Spanish, Haitian Creole or another language, and when.
- **Insurance and payment facts:** networks you are in, membership plans and financing, so pages and ads match reality.
- **Storm procedures:** who decides to close, who updates Google and the website, and how patients are told.
- **Access:** read-only access to Google Business Profile, Search Console, Analytics and Google Ads, and summary new-patient counts from the practice management system. Never patient records.

We start with the [Dental Growth Audit](/dental-marketing-audit/), which shows where inquiries are being lost before anything is spent on new campaigns.

Most Florida practices sit in, or compete with, one of four metropolitan areas. The labels matter, because public data is published for each geography separately and a statewide figure never describes a single city.

- **Miami and Fort Lauderdale share a metro area but not a market.** Miami is a city in Miami-Dade County; Fort Lauderdale is a city in Broward County. - **Tampa sits on one side of a bay.** The Tampa metro includes Pinellas County across Old Tampa Bay. ## Migration shapes how Florida patients look for a dentist

Florida grew by about 1.84 million people between April 2020 and April 2025, to 23,379,261. BEBR attributes all of that growth to net migration: deaths exceeded births by roughly 101,000 over the period. Only 35.5% of Florida residents were born in the state, according to the ACS.

<SourceNote ids={["bebr-florida-estimates-2025", "census-acs5-2024-b05002-birthplace"]} />

For marketing, the consequence is that a large share of potential patients have no existing local dentist and no word-of-mouth network. They search, compare Business Profiles and reviews, and try to book — often on a phone, often outside office hours. That puts weight on three things a practice controls:

1. **Being findable for the right services near the right places.** Google describes local results as based on relevance, distance and prominence. A newcomer searching from a new address will mostly see practices close to them, so accurate categories, services and hours on the profile matter as much as the website. Our [Google Business Profile work](/services/dental-local-seo-google-business-profile/) starts there.
2. **A booking path that works for someone with no prior relationship.** New residents need to know whether you take their insurance, how to book, and what happens next.
3. **Measurement that separates newcomers from existing patients.** If the practice management system records a referral source, use it; otherwise ask at booking.

## Seasonal residents: what the housing data shows and what it does not

Florida has many part-year residents, but no official survey counts seasonal dental patients by city. The closest public measure is housing: the ACS counts vacant units held "for seasonal, recreational, or occasional use". These are homes, not people, so they are a proxy for seasonal presence, nothing more.

| Geography | Seasonal-use units as a share of all housing units (ACS 2020–2024) |
|---|---|
| Fort Lauderdale city | 12.5% |
| Palm Beach County | 9.5% |
| Florida | 7.8% |
| Broward County | 7.2% |
| Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach metro | 6.8% |
| Tampa-St. Petersburg-Clearwater metro | 5.4% |
| Miami city | 4.2% |
| Orlando-Kissimmee-Sanford metro | 4.0% |
| Jacksonville metro | 2.7% |
| Jacksonville city | 0.7% |

<SourceNote ids={["census-acs5-2024-b25004-seasonal"]}>Shares calculated by Alpha Dental Media from Table B25004 (seasonal-use vacant units) and Table B25001 (all housing units). Margins of error apply.</SourceNote>

The spread is wide enough to change plans. A practice in a high-seasonal-housing area should test whether new-patient calls, cleanings and treatment starts rise in winter, and whether part-year residents need different information: records transfer, appointments timed around arrival and departure, and which out-of-state plans are accepted. A Jacksonville practice can largely ignore the question. Neither should move budget on the housing figure alone.

## Language at home differs more by region than anywhere else in the plan

Statewide, 30.6% of residents aged five and over speak a language other than English at home, and 22.6% speak Spanish. Those averages hide large regional differences.

|---|---|---|---|
| Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach | 55.2% | 43.2% | 24.4% |
| Orlando-Kissimmee-Sanford | 34.9% | 25.5% | 13.3% |
| Tampa-St. Petersburg-Clearwater | 23.1% | 15.5% | 8.9% |
| Jacksonville | 13.8% | 7.0% | 5.1% |

<SourceNote ids={["census-acs5-2024-dp-florida-regions"]} />

Two further details matter in South Florida. The ACS groups "French, Haitian, or Cajun" together, and that group is 7.1% of Broward County residents five and over and 6.7% in Palm Beach County; it does not isolate Haitian Creole. And language spoken at home does not tell you which language a person prefers for booking or treatment discussions. We treat language as a hypothesis to test with the practice's own intake data.

## Insurance and payment context

Insurance shapes what patients search for and whether they book. In the ACS, 11.5% of Floridians had no health insurance; dental coverage is not measured there, so this is context rather than a dental figure. The ADA Health Policy Institute classifies Florida's adult Medicaid dental benefit as emergency-only for 2021 through 2025, and reports that 25.6% of Florida dentists were enrolled as Medicaid or CHIP providers in 2024. Among Florida children continuously enrolled in Medicaid or CHIP, 40.7% visited a dentist in 2024; among privately insured children, 63.6% did in 2023.

<SourceNote ids={["census-acs5-2024-dp-florida-regions", "ada-hpi-medicaid-tables-2025"]} />

These are state figures and never describe a particular city. Their marketing implication is practical: pediatric practices that accept Medicaid face a very different demand and capacity picture from fee-for-service cosmetic practices, and both need pages that say plainly what is accepted. Ambiguity about insurance generates calls the front desk cannot convert and inquiries that never become visits.

## Florida rules that shape dental advertising

Florida regulates dental advertising more specifically than many states, and its definition of advertising includes websites and social media. The points that most often affect marketing copy:

- **Section 466.019, Florida Statutes**, prohibits misleading statements, including laudatory statements about the dentist, statements comparing the quality of services with other dentists, claims likely to create unjustified expectations of results, appeals primarily to fear, and fee information without a disclaimer that it is a minimum fee only. Advertising certain telehealth services requires a specified disclaimer.
- **Section 456.062** requires a specific capitalised statement in any advertisement for a free, discounted or reduced-fee service or examination.
- **Rule 64B5-4.002, Florida Administrative Code**, requires all advertising to identify the Florida-licensed dentist who takes responsibility for it, prohibits in-person and telephone solicitation (scheduling and follow-up with patients excepted), and restricts naming people who neither practise at the location nor own it. The rule was amended with effect from 24 September 2026.

<SourceNote ids={["fl-statutes-466-019", "fl-statutes-456-062", "fl-rule-64b5-4-002"]} />

In practice this rules out a good deal of common agency copy — "the most trusted implant team in the city", unqualified price headlines, free-exam banners without the statutory statement — and it is one reason we keep Florida pages factual and specific.

## Hurricane season as a communications and continuity issue

NOAA's National Hurricane Center gives the Atlantic hurricane season as June 1 to November 30, with a climatological peak around 10 September and most activity between mid-August and mid-October. For a practice, the marketing question is not forecasting; it is whether patients get accurate information when the office closes, and whether paid campaigns keep sending people to a closed office.

<SourceNote ids={["noaa-nhc-hurricane-climatology"]} />

Google's guidance draws a line that matters here: special hours are for temporary changes or closures of up to six days in a row; for seven days or more, or an unknown period, Google says to mark the business temporarily closed. Google Ads location assets stop showing for locations marked temporarily or permanently closed in Business Profile.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-special-hours", "google-ads-location-assets"]} />

## Multi-office groups and DSOs operating in Florida

Groups with offices in more than one Florida metro need consistent structure without identical messaging: one profile and one page per real office, shared brand standards, and reporting that compares each office with its own history before comparing offices.
