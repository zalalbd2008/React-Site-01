<KeyAnswer>The calculator multiplies your ad spend through a chain of your own conversion assumptions — clicks, inquiries, qualified inquiries, bookings, attended visits — and then divides total relevant cost (ad spend plus fees plus other channel costs) by the attended visits. The result is an estimated cost per attended new-patient visit. The starting values are illustrative, not benchmarks.</KeyAnswer>

## How to use it

1. **Start with spend and cost per click from your own account.** Monthly ad spend is what you pay the ad platform. For cost per click, use the average CPC from a recent, representative period in your Google Ads account; Google defines it as the total cost of clicks divided by the number of clicks.
2. **Enter the four conversion rates as percentages.** Each one applies to the stage before it, not to clicks. If 50% of qualified inquiries book, enter 50 in the booking field — not the percentage of all clicks that book.
3. **Add every cost tied to the channel.** The management fee goes in its own field. Call tracking, landing-page software and similar tools go in "other monthly cost".
4. **Optionally add revenue and margin.** Average collected revenue per attended new patient and the contribution margin on it unlock ROAS and profit-based ROI. Leave them blank if you do not have reliable figures.
5. **Change one assumption at a time.** The useful question is usually "which rate moves the result most?" — that is the one to measure properly first.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-avg-cpc"]} />

If your rates come from a period with tracking problems — calls not recorded, forms counted twice — the result inherits those problems. The [metrics glossary](/resources/dental-marketing-metrics/) defines each stage so that the numbers you enter mean the same thing every month.

## The formulas

Every output is calculated from the inputs above it. Rates are entered as percentages and divided by 100 before use.

Results are shown to one decimal place for counts below clicks, because fractional patients are a normal feature of averages: a monthly estimate with a decimal describes an expected rate over many months, not a part of a patient.

## Working through the illustrative defaults

<Illustrative title="The calculator's starting values, step by step">
With $3,000 of ad spend and an assumed $6 cost per click, the calculator estimates 500 clicks. An 8% inquiry rate gives 40 inquiries; 60% qualified gives 24 qualified inquiries; 55% booked gives 13.2 bookings; 80% attendance gives about 10.6 attended visits. Adding a $1,500 management fee makes total relevant cost $4,500. That is $187.50 per qualified inquiry and about $426 per attended new-patient visit.

Now add an assumed $800 of collected revenue per attended new patient and a 40% contribution margin. Estimated revenue is about $8,448, so ROAS is about 2.8×. But contribution margin is about $3,379, which is less than the $4,500 total cost, so profit-based ROI is about −25% for the month.

Every figure here is an illustrative placeholder, not a dental benchmark.
</Illustrative>

The second half of the example is the reason the calculator shows both return measures. A ROAS above 1 looks like the channel "makes money"; the profit view shows that, on these assumptions and in this one-month window, it does not cover its own cost.

## Ad spend versus agency fees

Ad spend is paid to the platform and buys clicks. A management fee is paid to whoever runs the account. The calculator keeps them in separate fields for three reasons:

- **Transparency.** You should always be able to see how much of the budget reached the ad platform. If a proposal bundles the two into one figure, ask for the split — the [agency comparison scorecard](/resources/dental-agency-comparison-scorecard/) treats this as a scored criterion.
- **Different behaviour.** Raising spend changes clicks; raising fees does not. Keeping them apart shows how the cost per attended visit responds to each.
- **Honest unit costs.** Cost per attended visit uses total relevant cost. A figure built from ad spend alone would understate what each patient cost the practice.

## ROAS versus profit-based ROI

ROAS divides revenue by ad spend only. Google Ads describes its version as conversion value per cost — the average conversion value, such as revenue, per dollar spent on ads — and that value is whatever the advertiser reports. Profit-based ROI subtracts total relevant cost from contribution margin, so it accounts for fees, tools and the direct cost of the treatment.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-target-roas"]} />

Use ROAS to compare campaigns inside one account where fees and margins are constant. Use profit-based ROI to decide whether the channel is worth funding. For the reasoning behind that distinction, read [how to tell leads, bookings, visits, revenue and profit apart](/blog/dental-marketing-roi/).

## Assumptions built into the model

- **A single average cost per click.** Real accounts have many CPCs; the average hides the mix. A shift toward more expensive terms changes the result even if the average you entered does not.
- **Stages are sequential and independent.** The model assumes each rate applies to the stage before it and does not change as volume changes. In practice, more spend often reaches less specific searches, so inquiry and qualification rates can fall as budget rises.
- **One month, one channel.** All inputs are monthly and refer to the same channel. Mixing a quarterly fee with monthly spend, or organic inquiries with paid costs, distorts every output.
- **New patients only.** Cost per attended visit describes new patients only if every input refers to new-patient activity. If your inquiry rate includes existing patients calling to reschedule, the output will look better than reality.
- **Capacity is not modelled.** The calculator will estimate any number of attended visits, including more than the schedule can absorb. Check new-patient appointment availability before acting on any increase.

## Limitations

The calculator does not account for revenue that arrives after the first visit, patient lifetime value, seasonality, conversion lag between click and appointment, or the effect of organic visibility and referrals on paid results. It does not model Google Ads auction dynamics; you cannot buy a fixed number of clicks at a fixed price. It cannot tell you what your rates should be — only what your rates imply. To set a budget from targets rather than estimate outcomes from a budget, see [setting a dental ad budget from qualified inquiries and attended visits](/blog/dental-google-ads-budget/).

## How missing values and zero denominators are handled

The calculator shows a dash rather than a number whenever the arithmetic would be meaningless:

- **Cost per click of zero.** Clicks cannot be estimated, so every downstream count shows a dash and the field displays an error.
- **Any rate outside 0–100, or a negative cost.** That field is flagged and the outputs that depend on it show a dash. An invalid cost is excluded from total relevant cost.
- **Zero attended visits or zero qualified inquiries.** The matching cost-per figure shows a dash instead of dividing by zero.
- **Zero ad spend.** ROAS shows a dash because there is no spend to divide by.
- **Zero total cost, or revenue or margin left blank.** Profit-based ROI and, where relevant, revenue and ROAS show a dash.

Blank optional fields are treated as "not provided", not as zero.

## Using the result

The most useful output is often not the final cost but the sensitivity: because the stages multiply, halving the booking rate halves attended visits — the same effect on cost per attended visit as doubling the cost per click, and fixing call handling requires no extra media spend.
