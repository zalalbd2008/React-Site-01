<KeyAnswer>Score each agency proposal 0, 1 or 2 on nine criteria: account ownership, fee transparency, ad spend separation, reporting definitions, case-study evidence, contract terms, access and offboarding, dental specialty knowledge, and compliance awareness. A 2 requires written evidence, not a verbal assurance. Treat a zero on ownership or offboarding as disqualifying, then compare the totals.</KeyAnswer>

This is the working tool. If you want the reasoning behind each question — why ownership matters, what to ask in the first call — read [what to ask a dental marketing agency](/blog/choosing-a-dental-marketing-agency/) first. The scorecard turns those questions into something you can fill in, compare and keep on file.

## How to score

- **0 — Missing or unfavourable.** The proposal says nothing, or says something that works against the practice.
- **1 — Stated but not evidenced.** The agency says the right thing in conversation or in general terms, but it is not in the proposal or contract.
- **2 — Committed in writing with evidence.** The proposal or draft contract commits to it, and you have seen the evidence described in the table.

Score from documents, not memories of a sales call. If an answer was only given verbally, it scores 1 at most until it appears in writing.

## Notes on each criterion

### Account ownership

Ownership decides whether the practice can see its own data and keep its history if the relationship ends. Google's platforms are built for this arrangement. In Google Ads, access levels run from email-only through billing, read-only and standard to admin, and Google warns that an account with only one administrator risks losing access if that person becomes unavailable. A Business Profile can have several owners but only one primary owner, and managers cannot add or remove users. In Google Analytics, users can be added at the account or property level, and only an Administrator can add, change or remove users. The practice should hold the top role in each and add the agency as a user.

### Fee transparency and ad spend separation

These are scored separately because they fail separately. A proposal can itemise its own fees clearly and still blend media into a single invoice. Ask each agency to show, for one sample month, exactly how much would reach the ad platform. The [growth calculator](/resources/dental-growth-calculator/) keeps the two apart for the same reason: spend buys clicks, fees pay for work, and the cost per attended visit needs both.

### Reporting definitions

A 2 here means the agency can say, in writing, what it will count as an inquiry and how it will learn whether that inquiry became an attended visit. The [metrics glossary](/resources/dental-marketing-metrics/) gives the definitions we use; an agency does not have to use ours, but it should have its own, and they should be specific.

### Case-study evidence

Results without a baseline, a period and a data source cannot be checked. A careful agency will also say what else changed during the period — a new dentist, extended hours, a price change — because those confounders explain part of any result. Absence of case studies is not automatically a zero; an honest "we cannot share client data, here is how we would measure yours" can score 1.

### Contract terms and offboarding

Read the termination clause before the scope. The most expensive contract is often the one you cannot leave. Offboarding should cover more than passwords: website files and content, the domain, call-tracking numbers, conversion tags and the documentation that explains them.

### Specialty knowledge and compliance awareness

Specialty knowledge shows up as questions, not claims. A proposal that asks about chair capacity, insurance participation and how calls are answered is reasoning about your practice; one that leads with a keyword list is reasoning about a template. Compliance awareness is similar. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews prohibits fake reviews, buying reviews of a particular sentiment and certain forms of review suppression. An agency should know this without being asked and should send legal questions to the practice's counsel rather than answer them itself.

## Weighting and totals

The unweighted maximum is 18. If some criteria matter more to you, double them before adding — for example, a dental group that has been through an acquisition may double ownership and offboarding. Decide the weights before you read the proposals, write them down, and apply them to every proposal the same way.

## A worked comparison

<Illustrative title="Two invented proposals for a two-office general practice">
Proposal A quotes one monthly figure covering "SEO, ads and reporting", sets up a new Google Ads account under the agency's login and offers a 12-month term with automatic renewal. Its sample report shows clicks, calls and "leads". It scores: ownership 0, fee transparency 0, spend separation 0, reporting 1, evidence 1, contract 0, offboarding 0, specialty 1, compliance 1 — a total of 4, with two disqualifying zeros.

Proposal B itemises SEO and ads management separately, has ad spend billed by the platform to the practice, will work in the practice's existing accounts, and offers a rolling monthly term after three months with 30 days' notice. Its sample report defines inquiries and asks for monthly booking data from the front desk. It has no case studies to share but explains its measurement method. It scores: ownership 2, fees 2, spend 2, reporting 2, evidence 1, contract 2, offboarding 1 (steps described verbally), specialty 2, compliance 1 — a total of 15.

Proposal B's fee is higher. Run through the growth calculator with the same assumptions, the difference in fee changes the cost per attended visit by a known amount; Proposal A's numbers cannot be checked at all. All details are invented for illustration.
</Illustrative>

## Using the result

The total is a starting point for negotiation, not a verdict. Take each 1 back to the agency and ask what it would take to make it a 2 in writing; how an agency responds to that request is itself evidence.
