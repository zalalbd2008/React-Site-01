<KeyAnswer>A dental startup should treat marketing in stages. Before opening, build the assets it will own: name and signage, phone system, website, Business Profile and tracking. Around opening, add tightly targeted paid search sized to real capacity. In the first year, shift spend toward reviews, retention and the services the practice wants to grow.</KeyAnswer>

## What makes a startup's marketing problem different

An established practice markets to grow; a startup markets to survive its ramp. The business model is front-loaded: lease, build-out, equipment and staff costs arrive before the first patient, usually financed by debt, and revenue builds month by month as the patient base forms. Every month the schedule sits partly empty extends the period in which the practice is spending more than it collects.

That changes three things about marketing:

- **Timing matters as much as channel choice.** Money spent on visibility before the practice can see patients is mostly wasted; money not spent in the first weeks after opening leaves chairs empty when the cost of an empty chair is highest.
- **Assets beat rentals.** A website, a correctly set-up Business Profile, a clean phone system and a review process keep working after a campaign stops. Paid media stops the day the budget does.
- **There is no history to optimise against.** A startup has no reviews, no ranking history, no call data and no record of which services patients ask for. The first months are as much about learning as acquiring.

New practices are also a minority path. In the ADA Health Policy Institute's 2025 data, only 14.8% of dentists up to ten years out of dental school worked in a one-location, one-dentist practice, compared with 32.6% of all dentists, and 27.1% of that early-career group were affiliated with a DSO. A startup owner is often competing against organisations that already have brand recognition, central call handling and established review profiles.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-hpi-practice-modalities-2025"]}>Counts of dentists by practice type, not counts of new practices opened. HPI notes a slight undercount of DSO affiliation.</SourceNote>

## Marketing decisions to make before the lease is signed

Several choices that look like real-estate or legal decisions are, in effect, marketing decisions. They are expensive or impossible to reverse once the practice opens.

- **Site and visibility.** Street frontage, signage rights, parking and how easily the office can be found on a map all affect how the practice performs in local search and how patients judge it. Google's guidelines expect a business showing its address on Google to maintain permanent fixed signage with its name at that address.
- **Name.** Google's guidelines say the profile name should reflect the business's real-world name as used consistently on the storefront, website and stationery, and that adding unnecessary information to the name is not permitted and can lead to suspension. A name chosen for search keywords rather than for the sign creates a problem that cannot be fixed later without a rebrand.
- **Phone number and website ownership.** The practice should own its domain, its main phone number and the accounts for its Business Profile, analytics and ads from day one. Google's eligibility rules say the profile's phone and website should be the single authoritative number and website for the business, verifiable by the owner, and that website content must be owned and managed by the business owner.
- **Service focus.** Decide which two or three service lines the practice wants to be known for. That decision shapes the website structure, the profile's categories and services, and which searches to target first.

## What Google allows before a practice opens

A startup can appear on Google before opening, within limits. Google lets a business that has not yet opened create a profile and set an opening date up to one year in the future; once the date is entered and the business is verified, the profile shows on Google 90 days before opening. Users cannot post reviews or photos, or suggest edits, until the business is officially open, and a "Recently opened" label shows for 90 days afterwards.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-opening-date"]} />

Verification is the step that most often slips. Google says the methods offered depend on business type, public information, region and hours, and some methods require showing the location, signage or equipment and proof that you manage the business. Reviews of verification can take up to five business days. Build signage installation and verification into the construction schedule rather than treating them as a last-week task.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-verify"]} />

The website follows a similar logic. Google's SEO Starter Guide notes that some changes take effect in a few hours and others take several months. A site launched the week the practice opens will not have had time to be discovered and understood; launching it with complete information well before opening gives it that time.

<SourceNote ids={["google-seo-starter-guide"]} />

## Budgeting for the ramp

A startup's marketing budget has two parts that behave differently:

- **A one-off build cost** before opening: website, photography of the finished office, phone system configuration, profile and listings setup, tracking. This is capital spent once and owned afterwards.
- **A variable acquisition budget** after opening, mostly paid search and, where it fits, local awareness. This should flex with capacity and results week by week.

The variable budget should be derived from the ramp plan, not from a percentage of projected revenue. Start with the number of new patients the schedule can take each month, estimate the share of inquiries that the team will book and that will attend, and work back to the inquiries needed. The [dental growth calculator](/resources/dental-growth-calculator/) lets you run those assumptions; for a startup, treat every rate as a guess to be replaced with your own data within the first two months.

One structural caution: the ADA Code of Ethics treats paying a marketing vendor a percentage of the professional fees collected from patients as fee splitting, and specifically includes "social media coupons" and influencers paid that way. Per-patient or revenue-share marketing deals that look attractive to a cash-constrained startup should be reviewed against the Code and your state's rules first.

## Capacity, staffing and the phones in a new office

A new practice's capacity is defined by who it has hired. If the practice opens with one hygienist, a part-time assistant and an owner who is also answering the phone between patients, the realistic number of new patients per week is lower than the business plan's target, and marketing should match it.

Staffing is not easy to scale quickly. In the ADA Health Policy Institute's Q3 2026 panel of 704 private-practice dentists, about one-third were recruiting hygienists or assistants, most of those recruiting hygienists found it very or extremely challenging, and about one in five reported hygienist positions open more than six months before being filled.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-hpi-dental-economy-q3-2026"]}>National self-reported panel; local labour markets vary.</SourceNote>

So before paid campaigns start, confirm:

- Who answers the phone at every hour the ads run, including lunch.
- What happens to calls and web requests when the office is closed.
- Whether the scheduler can see and offer the next open new-patient slot immediately.
- Whether the team knows which insurance plans the practice accepts, in the same words the website uses.

## Reviews, referrals and community in year one

Reviews take time for a startup, and there is no legitimate shortcut. Federal rules prohibit businesses from writing or buying fake reviews and from offering incentives conditioned on a review expressing a particular sentiment. A consistent, unconditional request process from the first week is the only durable route.

<SourceNote ids={["ftc-consumer-reviews-rule"]} />

Alongside reviews, a startup often builds its first patient base from relationships: local employers, community organisations, specialists who may refer back for general care, and the owner's existing professional network. These channels are hard to measure in an ad dashboard, so track them simply: ask every new patient how they heard about the practice, and record it in the practice-management system in a consistent set of categories.

## A worked example: a de novo practice's first year

<Illustrative title="A single-dentist startup opening in a growing suburb">
A dentist signs a lease in a new retail centre with roadside signage rights and plans to open in seven months with one hygienist and two front-office staff. The business plan targets 30 new patients a month by month six.

**Months 7 to 4 before opening:** the practice chooses a name that matches the planned sign, registers its domain and main phone number in the owner's name, and begins the website with full service, insurance and team information.

**Months 3 to 1 before opening:** the Business Profile is created with an opening date; verification is scheduled for the week after the exterior sign goes up. Tracking is set up for calls, forms and online bookings, and the front desk is trained on the phone script.

**Opening to month 3:** paid search runs on a small set of high-intent general and emergency searches within a short drive, only during staffed hours. Review requests go to every patient after their visit. Each week the team reviews call outcomes and cuts searches that produce calls but no bookings.

**Months 4 to 12:** with roughly 20 to 40 reviews and a few months of data, spend shifts toward the service the owner wants to grow, a new hygiene day is added once a second hygienist is hired, and recall systems begin to matter as the first patients become due.
</Illustrative>

All figures and timings above are invented for illustration.

## How a startup should measure progress

In the first months, totals are small and noisy. Track the funnel layers separately: inquiries, qualified inquiries (new patients the practice can serve), booked appointments, attended visits and, later, accepted treatment. Weekly reviews of call outcomes matter more than monthly ranking reports in this period, because the front desk is still learning and small fixes have large effects.

By month six, a startup should be able to say which sources produce attended new patients and at what cost per attended patient, using the definitions in our [dental marketing metrics reference](/resources/dental-marketing-metrics/). That is the point to decide whether to expand campaigns, add services or hold spend steady.
