## How a general practice turns marketing into revenue

A general practice earns most of its revenue from relationships that run for years. A new patient typically starts with an exam, cleaning and radiographs, joins a recall cycle, and generates restorative work as needs are found and accepted. The first visit is rarely where the value sits; the value is in whether the patient returns, accepts recommended care and refers family members.

That has three consequences for marketing:

1. **The right patient matters more than the cheapest lead.** A patient whose insurance the practice does not take, who lives an hour away, or who wants only a one-off cleaning is a weaker fit than a nearby family that will stay on recall.
2. **Retention and reputation are part of acquisition.** A practice that loses patients because recall reminders lapse, or that accumulates poor reviews about billing surprises, pays twice for growth.
3. **Capacity sets the ceiling.** New-patient marketing only produces revenue if there are hygiene and doctor appointments to put those patients into within a reasonable wait.

General practices also differ widely in case mix. Some run a mostly preventive, insurance-heavy schedule; others place implants, offer clear aligners, provide sedation or see a high share of emergency visits. The marketing question is not "how do we get more patients" but "which patients, for which chairs, at what time of day".

## What a general practitioner's competitive landscape looks like

General dentists compete in markets that mix solo offices, multi-dentist practices and large groups. In the ADA Health Policy Institute's 2025 data, 33.8% of general practitioners worked in a practice with one location and one dentist, and 16.3% were affiliated with a dental support organization. Across all dentists, DSO affiliation reached 27.1% among those up to ten years out of dental school.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-hpi-practice-modalities-2025"]}>These shares count dentists, not offices, and HPI notes its method slightly undercounts DSO and large-group affiliation.</SourceNote>

The practical reading is that a single-office general practice usually competes against at least one organisation with a central marketing team, call centre and larger ad budget. Matching that spend is rarely the right response. The single office can compete on things a large group finds harder to standardise: the specific dentist, continuity of care, the reputation of the front desk, and depth in a few services the owner chooses to emphasise.

## Why capacity comes before the channel decision

The first question in any general-practice engagement is how many new patients the schedule can absorb, and where. A practice with open doctor time but a fully booked hygiene schedule needs different marketing from one with the reverse.

National conditions in late 2026 point both ways. In the ADA Health Policy Institute's Q3 2026 panel of 704 private-practice dentists, 38% said they were not busy enough and could treat more patients, up from 26% the previous quarter, and owner dentists accepting new patients reported an average wait of about 12 business days for a new patient's first appointment. In the same report, about one-third of dentists were recruiting hygienists or dental assistants, most of those recruiting hygienists described it as very or extremely challenging, and about one in five reported hygienist positions open for more than six months before being filled.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-hpi-dental-economy-q3-2026"]}>Self-reported national panel; your market and practice may differ.</SourceNote>

So a practice may have demand capacity on the doctor's side and a staffing constraint on the hygiene side at the same time. If the bottleneck is an unfilled hygienist position, marketing for new cleanings and exams will lengthen the wait rather than grow revenue. In that case the better marketing investment may be recruitment visibility, recall reactivation, or promoting the restorative and elective services the doctor has time for.

Before choosing channels, write down:

- New-patient slots available per week, split by hygiene and doctor time.
- The current wait for a new-patient appointment and for an emergency visit.
- Hours the phones are actually staffed, including lunch and after hours.
- The insurance plans the practice is in network with, and any it plans to leave.
- The services the owner wants more of, and any the practice cannot currently accept.

## How insurance participation shapes the message

For many general practices, insurance participation is the single largest influence on who calls. A patient searching for a dentist often filters by plan before anything else, and a mismatch discovered on the phone wastes both the caller's time and the front desk's.

Marketing implications follow directly from the payer mix:

- **In-network practices** benefit from an accurate, plan-by-plan insurance page, clear wording about benefits verification, and consistent information across the website, Business Profile and phone scripts. "We accept most insurance" invites calls the practice cannot convert.
- **Practices moving toward fee-for-service** need to explain fees, financing or an in-house membership plan before they advertise, because the first objection will be cost. The website and the person answering the phone have to say the same thing.
- **Practices considering Medicaid** should check their own state's rules. Federal rules require states to provide dental benefits to children in Medicaid and CHIP, but states choose whether to cover adults and there is no minimum adult benefit, so the patients a participating general practice can serve, and the marketing that fits, depend on the state program.

<SourceNote ids={["medicaid-gov-dental-care"]} />

## Phones, front desk and the hand-off to the schedule

In a general practice, the person answering the phone converts more marketing than any campaign setting. Calls from search are short decisions: if the caller reaches voicemail, waits on hold, or is offered an appointment three weeks out without an alternative, many will try the next practice in the results.

A marketing plan for a general practice therefore includes the front desk:

- Which calls reach a person, and which go to voicemail, by hour of day.
- Whether the team can distinguish a new-patient call from an existing patient, a vendor or a wrong number.
- What happens to web form submissions after hours, and who follows up the next morning.
- Whether the scheduler can offer the next available new-patient slot without checking with someone else.

Measuring calls without counting every call as a new patient is its own discipline; our article on [measuring dental calls accurately](/blog/dental-call-tracking/) explains how to tag outcomes so that reports reflect booked and attended visits. Call recording raises consent and privacy obligations that the practice should confirm with its own counsel.

## Offering advanced services as a general dentist

Many general dentists provide implants, clear aligners, sedation or cosmetic treatment, and those services often carry the highest value per case. Marketing them is legitimate, but the wording matters.

The ADA Code of Ethics allows dentists to announce as specialists only in the recognized dental specialties and only when they meet the educational requirements, and its examples of false or misleading advertising include statements likely to create an unjustified expectation about results. Section 5.I adds that general dentists announcing services should avoid implying specialization and should state that the services are provided by general dentists. The National Commission currently recognizes 12 specialties, and "cosmetic dentistry" is not one of them. State dental boards may set stricter rules.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-code-of-ethics-2026", "ncrdscb-recognized-specialties"]} />

In practice that means describing the service, the dentist's real training and what a consultation involves, without "specialist" language unless it is accurate. On Google, a dentist is an individual practitioner who can have a profile in addition to the practice profile in some circumstances, but Google's guidelines say a practitioner should not have multiple profiles to cover different specializations.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

When elective or implant work becomes a major share of the practice's revenue, the marketing model starts to look like the one described on our page about [marketing cosmetic and implant practices](/who-we-help/cosmetic-and-implant-practices/): longer decision cycles, consultation-quality measurement and more content depth.

## A worked example: open doctor time, booked hygiene

<Illustrative title="A two-dentist general practice with a hygiene bottleneck">
A two-dentist suburban practice reports about 40 new patients a month and wants 60. The owner's first instinct is to increase ad spend.

Its numbers tell a different story. Hygiene is booked five weeks out because one of three hygienist positions has been vacant for four months. The doctors, however, have about 10 open hours a week. Call tracking shows 30% of calls between 12:00 and 13:30 go unanswered, and the insurance page lists two networks the practice left last year.

A capacity-led plan would: correct the insurance page and Business Profile details; cover the lunch hour on the phones; run recall reactivation to fill doctor time with overdue restorative work; promote one or two doctor-time services the owner wants to grow, such as crowns or implant consultations, on dedicated pages; and hold broad new-patient advertising until the hygiene position is filled.

Success in the first quarter would be measured as answered-call rate, doctor hours filled and treatment accepted, not total new-patient count, which the hygiene bottleneck caps regardless of spend.
</Illustrative>

## How a general practice should measure success

Keep four layers apart: **inquiries** (calls, forms, booking attempts), **qualified inquiries** (new patients the practice can serve), **booked and attended visits**, and **collected revenue** over time. Reports that stop at calls or clicks overstate results, because they include existing patients, wrong numbers and callers the practice cannot accept.

For a general practice the most useful monthly view is attended new-patient visits by source and by payer type, alongside capacity used.
