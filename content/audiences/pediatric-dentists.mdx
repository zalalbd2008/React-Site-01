<KeyAnswer>Pediatric dental marketing speaks to parents and to referrers, not to children. Its shape is set by payer mix (often including Medicaid and CHIP), by referral relationships with general dentists and physicians, and by after-school scheduling pressure. Build accurate coverage information, strong local visibility and reviews, and match demand to the hours the practice can actually offer.</KeyAnswer>

## How a pediatric practice's business model works

A pediatric dental practice typically sees a high volume of preventive and restorative visits for children from infancy to late adolescence, with many patients on regular recall. Families stay for years, and siblings often join, so a single parent's decision can bring several patients. At the other end, patients age out and transfer to general practices, which means the practice's base turns over steadily and new-patient flow has to keep pace.

Four features distinguish the model from a general practice:

- **The decision-maker is not the patient.** Parents and caregivers search, compare, book and pay. They judge the practice on practical criteria: insurance, location, appointment times, how the team handles anxious children, and what other parents say.
- **Payer mix is often heavier in public coverage.** For many pediatric practices, Medicaid and CHIP are a major share of patients, which affects revenue per visit, scheduling and how the practice describes itself.
- **Referrals matter more than in general dentistry.** General dentists, physicians and community programs send children for first visits, for care the referring office does not provide, and for children with special health care needs.
- **Time of day is a hard constraint.** School hours concentrate demand into late afternoons, and in many practices the after-school slots are the scarcest resource.

Pediatric practices also tend to be group practices rather than solo offices. In HPI's 2025 data, 21.1% of pediatric dentists worked in a one-location, one-dentist practice, compared with 33.8% of general practitioners, while 43.8% worked in a single location with two or more dentists and 14.3% in practices with two to four locations. DSO affiliation among pediatric dentists was 16.6%.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-hpi-practice-modalities-2025"]}>Counts of dentists by specialty; HPI notes a slight undercount of DSO and large-group affiliation.</SourceNote>

## Payer mix sets the marketing frame

Children's dental coverage nationally is split between private and public benefits. HPI reports that among children ages 0 to 18, 56% have private dental benefits, 37% have dental benefits through Medicaid or CHIP and 7% have none. In 2024, 64% of children with private dental insurance had at least one dental visit, compared with 42% of those with public insurance and 26% with no dental insurance.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-hpi-coverage-faq"]}>National estimates; the mix in any one practice's catchment can be very different and should be checked against the practice's own data.</SourceNote>

The federal framework explains why public coverage is so central for pediatric practices. Medicaid.gov states that states are required to provide dental benefits to children covered by Medicaid and CHIP; for Medicaid children this runs through the Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic and Treatment (EPSDT) benefit, and a referral to a dentist is required for every child in line with the state's periodicity schedule. States choose how separate CHIP programs provide dental coverage, and must post a listing of participating Medicaid and CHIP dental providers on InsureKidsNow.gov.

<SourceNote ids={["medicaid-gov-dental-care"]} />

On the provider side, HPI's December 2025 report found that 41% of U.S. dentists participated in Medicaid or CHIP as of 2024, and that pediatric dentists have by far the highest participation of all dental specialists. The same report notes that Medicaid fee-for-service reimbursement in most states falls far below 50% of dentist charges and 60% of private insurance reimbursement.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-hpi-medicaid-programs-2025"]}>Participation is measured by provider enrolment and does not reflect patient volume.</SourceNote>

For marketing, that creates a real business decision: how much of the schedule to allocate to publicly and privately insured new patients, and how to describe participation honestly. We do not advise on participation itself; that belongs to the owner and their advisers. Marketing should reflect the decision precisely, so parents who call are patients the practice can see.

## Speaking to parents without clinical claims

Parents read pediatric websites to answer practical questions. A strong site explains what happens at a first visit, how the team approaches nervous children, whether parents can accompany their child, what plans are accepted, what to bring, and what appointment times exist. It shows the real team and office. It does not promise outcomes.

Truthfulness rules apply as they do for any dental advertising: the ADA Code says advertising must not misrepresent in any material respect and gives as an example statements likely to create unjustified expectations about results. Pediatric dentistry is one of the 12 specialties recognized by the National Commission, and the Code limits specialist announcements to dentists who meet the education requirements, which matters for general practices that market heavily to families.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-code-of-ethics-2026", "ncrdscb-recognized-specialties"]} />

## Directory listings are a pediatric channel

For families with Medicaid or CHIP, plan and government directories are often the first stop, ahead of a general web search. Because states must list participating providers on InsureKidsNow.gov, and managed-care plans publish their own directories, a participating practice should audit those listings alongside its Business Profile: name, address, phone, accepted plans, ages seen and whether it is taking new patients.

<SourceNote ids={["medicaid-gov-dental-care"]} />

A mismatch between a plan directory and the practice's own website is a common source of wasted calls, and fixing it is unglamorous but direct work.

## Capacity, school hours and the front desk

After-school appointments are usually the constraint. Marketing that increases new-patient demand without regard to time of day can lengthen waits for the slots parents want while morning time stays unused. Before scaling acquisition, look at:

- New-patient wait time by time of day, not just overall.
- Whether morning or school-holiday availability is clearly offered on the website and by the phone team.
- How quickly web requests are returned, since many parents submit forms in the evening.
- Whether the team can answer coverage questions on the first call.

Phone handling matters particularly because parents often call between other obligations. A missed call at 15:30 may not be repeated.

## Reviews and privacy in a pediatric setting

Parents rely heavily on other parents' reviews. A consistent request process works better than occasional pushes, and federal rules prohibit fake reviews and incentives conditioned on a particular sentiment, so the process must be unconditional.

<SourceNote ids={["ftc-consumer-reviews-rule"]} />

Responses need extra care because the patient is a child. Reply in general terms without confirming the family is a patient or describing any care. Our articles on [requesting reviews without incentives or gating](/blog/dental-review-request-process/) and [responding to negative dental reviews without disclosing patient information](/blog/responding-to-negative-dental-reviews/) cover both steps.

## Business Profiles for pediatric offices and practitioners

Many pediatric practices have several dentists and, often, more than one office. Google's guidelines allow an organisation profile per location and, where several public-facing practitioners work there, separate practitioner profiles named only with the practitioner's name; a practitioner should not have multiple profiles to cover different specializations. Practices with offices inside a larger general group should check that each pediatric office's name and category reflect how it actually presents itself.

## A worked example: a full afternoon, an empty morning

<Illustrative title="A three-dentist pediatric practice with uneven demand">
A pediatric practice with three dentists sees about 90 new patients a month, roughly half with Medicaid or CHIP coverage. Its after-school slots are booked six weeks out; morning slots on most weekdays are half empty.

The website does not mention morning availability, the Business Profile lists two dentists who have left, and one managed-care directory shows an old phone number. Web requests submitted after 18:00 are answered the next afternoon.

A plan for the next quarter: correct the directory and Business Profile; add a scheduling page that explains morning and school-holiday options; return evening web requests first thing each morning; and refresh referral information for local pediatric clinics. Paid search would be held until morning utilisation improves, then tested on searches near the office. Success would be measured as morning slots filled, new-patient wait for after-school times and calls from directory listings that book.
</Illustrative>

## How a pediatric practice should measure marketing

Report new patients by source, payer type and time-of-day booked, alongside the wait for after-school new-patient appointments. Separate inquiries from booked and attended first visits, and record referral source for every child, including referring office or clinic.
