<KeyAnswer>Marketing for cosmetic and implant practices should be organised around a long, high-value decision. Fill limited consultation time with serious candidates, explain cost and process honestly, follow up deliberately for weeks or months, keep claims within ethics and FTC rules, and judge results on consultations attended, cases accepted and collected revenue over a long enough window.</KeyAnswer>

## How a high-value elective practice makes money

A practice built around implants, full-arch restoration, veneers or other elective cosmetic work has a different economic shape from a general practice. Revenue is concentrated in fewer, larger cases. Many are paid largely out of pocket or with financing, with insurance covering little or none. A single accepted full-arch case can be worth as much as a large number of routine visits, and a single lost consultation can be expensive.

That changes almost every marketing decision:

- **The constraint is doctor and consultation time.** The dentist, and often a treatment coordinator, must spend significant time with each candidate. Marketing that fills consultation slots with people who are not yet serious crowds out those who are.
- **Case acceptance is the decisive step.** Between the consultation and the start there is a treatment plan, a cost, often a financing application, and time. Because each case is large, a decision lost at this stage costs far more than a lost click or call.
- **Cash flow and capacity extend beyond the chair.** Surgical scheduling, lab time and the practice's own appetite for large cases all limit how many starts the practice can take each month.

Not every practice in this category is a general practice. Implant surgery is also performed by specialists such as oral and maxillofacial surgeons and periodontists, and restoration by prosthodontists; many general dentists provide implants and cosmetic treatment too. In HPI's 2025 data, 24.7% of oral and maxillofacial surgeons were DSO-affiliated, compared with 11.3% of prosthodontists and 16.3% of general practitioners, so the competitive set varies by specialty and market.

## Why the decision cycle changes everything

Elective and implant decisions are high-consideration. People research, compare several practices, discuss cost with family, apply for financing and sometimes wait for a change in circumstances. The contrast with routine or urgent dental marketing is sharp.

Economic conditions add a further layer. HPI's Q3 2026 State of the Dental Economy report said consumer spending on dental care was down 2.4% so far in 2026, and suggested this may reflect fewer patients visiting, fewer patients accepting treatment plans and lower reimbursement. In the same quarter, 38% of the 704 private-practice dentists surveyed said they were not busy enough.

For an elective practice, that environment rewards follow-up and financing clarity more than raising ad spend.

The middle of the pipeline deserves as much attention as the top: getting qualified requests to attend, and getting attended consultations to a decision. A named person responsible for follow-up, a clear written summary of the plan and cost, and a schedule of check-ins after the consultation are operational choices that marketing depends on.

## Content depth and honest cost information

Because the decision is slow and high-stakes, people read more. A useful cosmetic or implant site explains what the process involves in plain terms, what the consultation covers and that suitability is assessed by the dentist there, how cost is structured, what financing exists, and who the dentist is. Separate pages for each major service line let the practice answer each decision properly and measure each separately.

Cost is the hardest content decision. Many practices avoid stating any figures; others advertise a low "from" price that few cases match. Honest middle ground, such as explaining what drives the cost and when a firm quote is given, is more likely to reduce consultations that end at the price without creating expectations the practice cannot meet. Our article on [implant advertising and consultation quality](/blog/dental-implant-advertising/) covers landing pages and how to show cost and financing in ads.

## What you can and cannot claim

Elective dentistry invites strong claims about results and expertise, and this is where ethics and federal rules matter most.

**Specialist wording.** Cosmetic dentistry is not one of the 12 dental specialties recognized by the National Commission. The ADA Code permits specialist announcements only in recognized specialties by dentists who meet the education requirements, and expects specialists to avoid implying that associated general practitioners are specialists.

<SourceNote ids={["ncrdscb-recognized-specialties", "ada-code-of-ethics-2026"]} />

**Results claims.** The ADA Code's examples of false or misleading advertising include statements likely to create an unjustified expectation about results, and unsubstantiated claims that services are superior to other dentists'. Federal endorsement guidance says an ad featuring a consumer's experience is likely to be read as representative of what consumers generally achieve; without substantiation, the ad should clearly disclose the generally expected results.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-code-of-ethics-2026", "ftc-endorsement-guides"]} />

**Reviews.** Federal rules prohibit businesses from writing, buying or knowingly disseminating fake reviews and testimonials, and from offering incentives conditioned on a review expressing a particular sentiment.

<SourceNote ids={["ftc-consumer-reviews-rule"]} />

**Vendor payment.** The ADA Code treats paying a marketing vendor a specific amount or percentage of the professional fees collected from patients as fee splitting. Per-case lead arrangements are sometimes offered to elective practices, so check any such proposal against the Code and state rules.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-code-of-ethics-2026"]} />

State dental boards may impose stricter advertising rules than any of these; the practice should confirm with its own counsel.

## Targeting limits in paid media

Many elective-marketing playbooks rely on retargeting people who visited a treatment page. That may not be available. Google lists Health among its sensitive interest categories in personalized advertising, and says advertisers promoting products and services in those categories cannot use advertiser-curated audiences such as customer match and their own data segments (formerly remarketing lists), although predefined Google audiences remain available. The page does not name dental services specifically, so plan as though the restriction may apply.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-personalized-restricted"]} />

The practical response is to rely more on search intent, specific treatment pages, and the practice's own follow-up with people who have already made contact, using channels they have agreed to receive.

## Measurement when cases close months later

Elective marketing is where weak measurement does most damage. If reports stop at leads, a campaign that produces many cheap, unqualified requests looks better than one that produces fewer serious candidates. If reports look only at the current month, a good campaign can look poor because its consultations have not yet accepted.

What works:

- Record the source of every consultation in the practice-management or CRM system, and carry it through to accepted cases and collected revenue.
- Report consultation attendance and case acceptance by source, over a window long enough for most consultations to resolve.
- Separate marketing cost from clinical and lab cost when judging profitability.
- Note case size; a source that produces fewer, larger cases may outperform one with more single-tooth cases.

Our article on [telling leads, bookings, visits, revenue and profit apart](/blog/dental-marketing-roi/) sets out these distinctions, and the [growth calculator](/resources/dental-growth-calculator/) can model how attendance and acceptance rates change the case count a budget produces.

## A worked example: busy consultations, few starts

<Illustrative title="An implant-focused practice with a follow-up gap">
A practice that places and restores implants runs about 50 implant consultations a quarter from paid search and its website, and accepts about 10 cases. The owner wants to double the ad budget.

The audit finds that consultation requests are answered quickly, but no one is assigned to follow up after the consultation; plans are presented verbally with no written summary; and financing is mentioned only if the patient asks. About a third of consultation requests come from outside a realistic travel distance and rarely attend. Reports count all form submissions as leads.

A plan for the next two quarters: narrow the ad area to a realistic catchment; give one coordinator responsibility for post-consultation follow-up at set intervals; provide a written plan summary and financing options at every consultation; track source through to accepted case; and hold the budget flat until acceptance improves. Success would be judged over six months on accepted cases and collected revenue per marketing dollar, not on lead count.
</Illustrative>
