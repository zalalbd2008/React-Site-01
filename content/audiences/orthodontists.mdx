<KeyAnswer>Orthodontic marketing should be planned around case starts, not leads, and around two sources of patients: referring dentists and direct searchers. Protect and measure referral relationships, give parents and adults separate, specific answers, and track every patient from consultation request to attended consult to start, by source.</KeyAnswer>

## How an orthodontic practice's revenue model shapes its marketing

Orthodontics is a specialty with a distinctive revenue pattern. A practice earns most of its income from comprehensive treatment cases that run over many months, often paid through a down payment and monthly instalments. A new patient is a consultation first, sometimes an observation patient for a period, and only then a treatment start. A comprehensive case is worth far more than a single routine visit, which makes each start valuable and each lost consultation costly.

Three features follow:

- **The unit of success is the start.** Consultation requests are an intermediate step. A practice can double inquiries and see no growth if the new inquiries are early researchers or people who cannot proceed.
- **Decisions involve more than one person.** For children and teenagers, a parent usually decides and pays, while the patient's preferences matter. For adults, the patient decides but often compares several options over weeks.
- **Capacity is measured in chair time and starts per month.** Many orthodontic practices see high volumes of short adjustment visits, so the constraint is often new-patient consultation slots and the time available for records and starts, not total appointments.

## What the orthodontic market looks like

Orthodontists are more consolidated than general dentists in some respects and more independent in others. In HPI's 2025 data, 24.4% of orthodontists were affiliated with a dental support organization, compared with 16.3% of general practitioners, while 36.6% worked in a one-location, one-dentist practice, a higher share than for general practitioners (33.8%).

<SourceNote ids={["ada-hpi-practice-modalities-2025"]}>Counts of dentists by specialty; HPI notes a slight undercount of DSO and large-group affiliation.</SourceNote>

That combination means many orthodontists are solo owners competing with group-owned orthodontic offices on one side and with general practices that provide clear aligners on the other. The marketing response differs depending on which of those competitors is taking cases in a given market.

## Referral relationships are a marketing channel

For many orthodontic practices, general dentists are the most important source of new patients. A referral arrives with trust already attached: the family's own dentist has recommended the practice. That makes referral relationships a channel to manage with the same care as search or advertising, even though they never appear in an ad dashboard.

What strengthens referral relationships is largely operational:

- Fast, reliable scheduling of referred patients, so the referring dentist's recommendation is not undermined by a long wait.
- Clear, timely communication back to the referring office, handled within the practice's privacy obligations.
- A simple way for referring offices to send patients, such as a referral form on the website that works on a phone.
- Recognition that some general practices now offer aligners themselves, which changes what they refer and when.

Direct-to-patient marketing can affect those relationships. Advertising messages that position the practice against general dentists, or campaigns that target the same patients a referring office is treating, risk being read as competition. The aim is direct marketing that complements referrals: speaking to people who have not yet been referred, rather than competing for those who have.

## Speaking to parents and to adults

Orthodontic marketing usually has two audiences whose questions differ enough to need separate treatment on the website and in campaigns.

**Parents** typically want to know when a first evaluation is appropriate, what treatment involves for school and activities, how many appointments to expect, what the total cost and payment options are, and whether insurance helps. Appointment times outside school hours are a practical concern. Content should answer these plainly, without clinical claims about outcomes.

**Adults** often research longer and compare options including aligners from general dentists. They ask about appearance during treatment, total cost, time commitment and what the consultation involves. Many will request a consultation online rather than call, so the form or booking path matters; our article on [online booking versus request forms](/blog/dental-online-booking-vs-request-forms/) discusses the tradeoff.

Separate service pages and separate ad groups for these audiences let the practice measure which produces starts, rather than averaging two very different patterns.

## Specialist status and truthful advertising

Orthodontics and dentofacial orthopedics is one of the 12 specialties recognized by the National Commission on Recognition of Dental Specialties and Certifying Boards. The ADA Code of Ethics allows dentists who meet the educational requirements to announce as specialists in a recognized specialty, using "specialist in" wording, and places the burden on specialists to avoid implying that general practitioners associated with them are also specialists.

<SourceNote ids={["ncrdscb-recognized-specialties", "ada-code-of-ethics-2026"]} />

That gives a qualifying orthodontist a truthful point of difference to state clearly. It does not license comparative claims about other providers: the ADA Code lists unsubstantiated claims that services are superior to those of other dentists among its examples of false or misleading statements, and state boards may impose further rules.

Before-and-after images and patient stories are common in orthodontic marketing. Federal endorsement guidance says an ad featuring a consumer's experience is likely to be read as representing what consumers generally achieve; if the advertiser cannot substantiate that, the ad should clearly disclose the generally expected results. Use images and testimonials only with written patient authorisation.

## Satellite offices, practitioners and Google

Orthodontic practices often run a main office plus satellites open on specific days. Google's eligibility rule is that a business must make in-person contact with customers during its stated hours, so each satellite's profile should show the hours it is actually staffed. Google's guidelines also address practitioners: where several public-facing practitioners work at a location, the organisation has its own profile and practitioners can have separate profiles, but a practitioner should not have multiple profiles to cover different specializations.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-eligibility", "google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

Getting this right matters for families who search near home or school and choose the office they can reach on the days they can attend.

## Insurance, payment plans and Medicaid

Most orthodontic marketing has to explain cost early, because cost is one of the first questions. A clear page on how fees are structured, what payment plans exist and how insurance benefits are applied reduces consultations that end at the price.

Medicaid's role varies. Federal rules require states to provide dental benefits to children in Medicaid through the EPSDT benefit, under which services must be provided if determined medically necessary, and states determine medical necessity. Orthodontic coverage under Medicaid therefore depends on each state's criteria. HPI reports that 41% of U.S. dentists participated in Medicaid or CHIP as of 2024, with participation varying widely by state and specialty.

<SourceNote ids={["medicaid-gov-dental-care", "ada-hpi-medicaid-programs-2025"]} />

A participating practice should describe eligibility cautiously and route those inquiries to staff who know the state rules; a non-participating practice should say so plainly to avoid consultations it cannot convert.

## A worked example: referrals softening in a competitive market

<Illustrative title="A two-office orthodontic practice with declining referrals">
An orthodontist with a main office and one satellite records about 40 starts a quarter. Historically, about 70% came from dentist referrals.

Over a year, referral-sourced consultations fall by about a fifth. A review by referring office shows that most of the drop comes from four general practices, two of which now offer aligners. Direct inquiries are steady, but the satellite's Business Profile lists the main office's full-week hours although it opens two days a week, and calls on the other days go unanswered.

A plan for the next two quarters: correct the satellite's hours; add separate parent and adult treatment pages; make the referral form work on a phone; arrange conversations with the four referring offices about which cases they refer; and run a small paid search test aimed at adults within the satellite's catchment, measured on attended consults and starts. Referral volume by office would be reviewed monthly.

## How an orthodontic practice should measure marketing

Keep these stages apart in reporting: consultation requests, consultations scheduled, consultations attended, treatment starts and, over time, collected revenue. Record the source of every new patient, including referring office, and review consult-to-start conversion by source and by office. A channel that produces many requests but few starts may still be worth it if its cost is low, but you can only tell if starts are tracked.
