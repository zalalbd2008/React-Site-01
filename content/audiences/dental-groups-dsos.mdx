## How a dental group's business model changes the marketing problem

A dental group earns its return by running more chairs on shared infrastructure: central billing, purchasing, HR, technology and, usually, marketing. That is close to how the ADA Health Policy Institute defines DSO affiliation: a practice is DSO-affiliated if an outside entity manages some or all of its non-clinical functions, such as billing, marketing or human resources.

The group's marketing therefore has two jobs that a single practice does not:

1. **Make the shared function efficient** — one set of standards, accounts and reports rather than ten different vendors and ten definitions of a "new patient".
2. **Keep each office competitive locally** — because patients still choose a specific office, near them, that takes their insurance and has a dentist they trust.

The tension between those two jobs explains most problems we look for in a group: central standards that ignore local reality, or local autonomy that makes results impossible to compare.

## How large is the group and DSO segment

Consolidation is uneven but significant. In HPI's 2025 data, 17.0% of U.S. dentists were affiliated with a DSO, rising to 27.1% of dentists up to ten years out of dental school and 31.4% of those up to five years out. By specialty, affiliation was 16.3% for general practitioners, 24.4% for orthodontists and 16.6% for pediatric dentists.

Account ownership deserves particular attention. Google's eligibility rules say only owners or authorised representatives may manage a Business Profile, that representatives must never claim a profile without the owner's consent, and that they must transfer ownership to the business owner on request.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-eligibility"]} />

In practice, the group should be the primary owner of every profile, ad account and analytics property, with vendors and office managers as managers. Acquisitions can arrive with profiles owned by a former owner's personal account or a previous agency; resolving that is an early task, not a later one. How profile roles and business groups work in Google's tools is covered on our [multi-location SEO service page](/services/local-seo-multi-location-clinics/).

## Brand architecture: one brand, many brands, or both

Groups grow in different ways, and the brand decision usually follows the growth path:

- **De novo growth** (opening new offices) usually favours a single group brand, since there is no existing local reputation to protect.
- **Acquisition growth** often inherits strong local names, reviews and patient loyalty. Many groups keep the acquired name, co-brand for a period, or move offices to the group brand in stages.
- **Specialty or affinity brands** (for example, pediatric or orthodontic offices within a general group) may justify separate names if they genuinely operate as distinct practices.

Google's guidelines for chains say all locations should share the same name unless the business's real-world representation consistently varies by location, and allow distinct names for sub-brands applied consistently. The rule that matters is consistency with the sign on the door.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

## Allocating budget between offices

Equal budgets per office are simple and usually wrong. A mature office with a full hygiene schedule and strong reviews does not need the same spend as a new office with open chairs, and an office in a dense, competitive area may need more spend to produce the same number of attended patients.

A more defensible allocation uses three inputs for each office:

1. **Open capacity** — new-patient slots available in the coming month, by hygiene and doctor time.
2. **Cost per attended new patient** — by channel, over a long enough period to be stable.
3. **Strategic priority** — new offices in ramp, offices adding a provider, or offices the group plans to expand.

Budget moves toward offices with open capacity and acceptable cost per attended patient, and away from offices that are full or where extra spend is no longer producing attended visits. That requires tracking that splits spend and outcomes by office, which is why measurement is a central function.

Current conditions make capacity-led allocation more important. In HPI's Q3 2026 panel of 704 private-practice dentists, 38% reported being not busy enough and able to treat more patients, while about one-third were recruiting hygienists or assistants and many reported hiring difficulty. A group can easily have offices in both situations at once.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-hpi-dental-economy-q3-2026"]}>National panel of private-practice dentists; not specific to groups.</SourceNote>

## Payer mix and service lines across a portfolio

Offices in the same group often serve different payer mixes. HPI reports that 49% of DSO-affiliated dentists participated in Medicaid or CHIP in 2024, down from 67% in 2015, compared with about two in five dentists not affiliated with a DSO.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-hpi-medicaid-programs-2025"]}>Participation means enrolment as a provider; it does not measure patient volume.</SourceNote>

Marketing has to reflect each office's actual participation. A group-wide message such as "we accept most insurance" can be accurate at one office and wrong at another, and central call agents need the office-level list. The same applies to services: if only some offices provide implants, orthodontics or sedation, location pages, Business Profile services and ad campaigns must say so office by office. Specialist wording is a compliance point: the ADA Code limits specialist announcements to recognized specialties and qualified dentists, and says specialists must avoid implying that associated general practitioners are specialists.

<SourceNote ids={["ada-code-of-ethics-2026"]} />

## Call handling at group scale

Groups commonly centralise phones to extend hours and standardise scripts. A central team is only as good as the information it can see: each office's live schedule, its insurance list, which dentists see children, which offices offer same-day emergency care.

Measure call handling by office and by who answered: answered rate, share of new-patient calls that booked, and attendance of those bookings. Compare central and local handling on the same measures before deciding. Our article on [measuring calls without counting every call as a new patient](/blog/dental-call-tracking/) explains outcome tagging.

## A worked example: an eight-office group after two acquisitions

<Illustrative title="Reallocating budget across an eight-office group">
A regional group has eight offices: five on the group brand and three acquired in the last year under their original names. Each office receives the same monthly paid-search budget.

The audit finds that two acquired offices' Business Profiles are still owned by the previous owner's personal account, one acquired office's website points patients to an old phone number, and tracking counts all calls as new patients. Once calls are tagged, cost per attended new patient ranges from roughly 120 to 600 across offices.

The group's three fullest offices have new-patient waits of four weeks or more. Two newer offices have open hygiene time most days.

The resulting plan: transfer profile ownership to the group first; fix the old phone number; adopt one call-outcome standard; move about a third of paid budget from the full offices to the two newer ones; and keep the acquired names for now, with a co-branding review in six months once reviews and rankings are measured.
</Illustrative>

All figures above are invented for illustration.

## Reporting that supports decisions

A group report should help decide where the next dollar goes. That means office-level rows, capacity alongside marketing results, and context columns such as office age, payer mix and competition. Raw totals will always favour mature offices in less competitive areas.
