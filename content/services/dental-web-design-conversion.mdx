## Why dental websites lose patients they have already won

Most practices do not have a traffic problem as much as a completion problem. Someone searches, finds the practice, taps through on a phone, and then stalls: the phone number is buried in a menu, the booking widget loads slowly or asks for an account, the implant page says nothing about cost or consultations, the insurance page lists plans no one has checked in years, or a sticky banner covers the form's submit button. None of these show up in a ranking report. All of them lose patients who were ready to act.

Our recommendation, before any talk of redesign, is to find out where real visitors stall and fix those points in order of how many patients they cost. Sometimes that leads to a new site. More often it leads to a focused set of changes to the booking path, the key treatment pages, speed and accessibility, done on the site you already have.

The question of why visitors do not book is explored in depth in our [mobile conversion audit article](/blog/dental-website-conversion-audit/). This page describes the service that acts on the answer.

## How we diagnose a dental website

### Task tests on real phones

We start with tasks, not page reviews. On a mid-range phone, on a mobile connection, we try to do what a new patient would: find whether the practice takes a given insurance plan, book a cleaning for a new patient, request an emergency appointment, find the cost range for a consultation, call from the treatment page. Each task is recorded with the number of steps, the points of hesitation and where it failed.

### Field performance, not just lab scores

We read real-user Core Web Vitals data where it exists for the site, and use lab tools to diagnose causes. Google's guidance is to assess the 75th percentile of page loads, separately for mobile and desktop, and lab tools cannot measure Interaction to Next Paint because no real user is interacting.

<SourceNote ids={["webdev-core-web-vitals"]} />

### Accessibility review

We review key templates and the booking path against WCAG 2.2 Level AA with automated checks plus manual keyboard and screen-reader testing. Automated tools catch only part of the picture, so the manual pass matters most on forms and booking widgets.

### Content and decision support

We check whether treatment, insurance and new-patient pages answer the questions people actually ask before booking, and whether those answers are current. A page that hides cost entirely and a page that quotes a figure nobody at the front desk recognises both erode trust.

### Form and booking routing

We submit test inquiries and follow them: where does the email go, who reads it, how fast, and does anything confirm to the patient that it arrived. A form that delivers to an unmonitored inbox converts visitors into lost patients.

### URL and technical inventory

If a redesign is on the table, we crawl the current site and pull its pages from Search Console so every URL with traffic or links is known before anything moves.

The [dental website audit checklist](/resources/dental-website-audit-checklist/) is the working version of these checks, if you want to run a first pass yourself.

## Mobile booking paths

A booking path is every step between a person deciding to contact you and the practice confirming the appointment. On a phone, each extra step, slow load or ambiguous label costs some share of visitors.

What we design for:

- **A persistent, honest primary action.** Call and book controls that stay reachable without covering content or form fields, labelled with what will happen ("Request an appointment", not "Get started").
- **Paths by visit type.** New patients, emergencies and consultations need different information and sometimes different routes. Emergency visitors need hours and a phone number immediately; implant consult visitors may want to read first.
- **Forms that ask only what the front desk needs.** Each field is justified by a real use at the practice, and nothing in the form invites visitors to type clinical details or insurance member IDs into an unsecured email.
- **Clear confirmation.** After submitting, the visitor learns what happens next and when.
- **Correct hours everywhere.** The website, Business Profile and phone message should agree, especially around holidays.

Whether to put live online booking in that path, or a request form, or both, is its own decision. Our article on [online booking versus appointment request forms](/blog/dental-online-booking-vs-request-forms/) covers it.

## Accessibility to WCAG 2.2 AA

We build and remediate to WCAG 2.2 Level AA, the current W3C Recommendation, published on 12 December 2024. W3C advises using 2.2 to maximise future applicability, and content that conforms to 2.2 also conforms to 2.0 and 2.1.

<SourceNote ids={["w3c-wcag-22"]} />

Several criteria added in 2.2 bear directly on dental booking paths:

- **Target Size (Minimum), 2.5.8 (AA):** pointer targets at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, with listed exceptions. Small date pickers and close buttons often fail.
- **Focus Not Obscured (Minimum), 2.4.11 (AA):** a keyboard user's focused element should not be hidden, for example by a sticky "Book now" bar or cookie banner.
- **Accessible Authentication (Minimum), 3.3.8 (AA):** relevant to patient portals and booking systems that require login.
- **Redundant Entry, 3.3.7 (A):** do not make people retype information already given in the same process.
- **Consistent Help, 3.2.6 (A):** contact options appear in a consistent place across pages.
- **Dragging Movements, 2.5.7 (AA):** sliders and drag interactions need a single-pointer alternative.

<SourceNote ids={["w3c-wcag-22"]} />

### What the Department of Justice says

On the retrieval date, the Department of Justice's published guidance on web accessibility stated that Title III of the ADA covers businesses open to the public, listed medical offices among its examples, and said such businesses must make the goods and services they provide online accessible. It also said the Department has no regulation setting detailed web standards for businesses, that businesses have flexibility in how they comply, and it named WCAG as helpful technical guidance. The guidance is dated 18 March 2022 and describes itself as non-binding.

<SourceNote ids={["doj-ada-web-guidance"]} />

We treat that as a reason to build accessibly, not as a legal conclusion. We do not certify compliance, and third-party booking, chat and review widgets can introduce barriers outside our control. Your own counsel should advise on legal exposure; our role is to make the site work for people using assistive technology and to document what was tested.

## Speed and Core Web Vitals

Google's published good thresholds are a Largest Contentful Paint within 2.5 seconds, an Interaction to Next Paint of 200 milliseconds or less, and a Cumulative Layout Shift of 0.1 or less, each assessed at the 75th percentile of page loads. A page passes when it meets all three.

<SourceNote ids={["webdev-core-web-vitals"]} />

Google recommends achieving good Core Web Vitals for success with Search and for user experience generally, but it is equally clear that page experience is not the whole story: its systems still show the most relevant content even when page experience is sub-par. For a practice, speed is mainly a conversion issue. A slow booking widget or a layout that jumps as the visitor taps loses appointments regardless of rankings.

<SourceNote ids={["google-core-web-vitals-search", "google-page-experience"]} />

Common causes on dental sites are oversized hero images and videos, chat and review widgets loading before content, several tracking scripts competing on the main thread, and fonts that shift text as they load. We fix causes and confirm the effect in field data over the following weeks, since field data lags changes.

## Content that helps people decide

A treatment page is a decision aid, not a brochure. The table sets out what we expect key pages to answer, and who has to supply the facts.

| Page | Questions it should answer | Who supplies the facts |
|---|---|---|
| New patient | What happens at the first visit, what to bring, how long it takes, how to book | Practice manager |
| Insurance and payment | Which plans are accepted or in network, how out-of-network claims work, payment and financing options | Billing lead, kept current |
| Treatment pages | Who it suits in general terms, what the consultation involves, what drives cost, how to arrange a consult | Dentist reviews clinical statements; practice confirms fees |
| Emergency | Hours, how to reach the practice now, what to do out of hours | Practice manager |
| Team | Who practises here, their roles and qualifications as the practice states them | Practice, verified |

On cost, we help practices say something honest without quoting numbers they cannot stand behind: what a consultation costs if anything, what makes the treatment cost more or less, and whether financing is available. Clinical claims are reviewed and approved by your dentists; we do not provide clinical review. Where a practice needs more than core pages, our [dental content marketing](/services/dental-content-marketing/) service takes over.

## Migrations without losing visibility

If URLs change, Google treats it as a site move. Its guidance is to prepare a mapping from old to new URLs, implement server-side permanent redirects (301 or 308), avoid redirect chains, update internal links and sitemaps, verify both old and new sites in Search Console, use the Change of Address tool when the domain changes, and keep redirects for as long as possible, generally at least one year. Google also says to expect temporary ranking fluctuation, and that medium-sized sites can take a few weeks or more to show new URLs in place of old ones.

<SourceNote ids={["google-site-move-url-changes"]} />

We run migrations to that standard and add dental-specific checks: Business Profile website links updated, booking and call-tracking numbers carried across, and old treatment pages with links pointed to their closest equivalent rather than the home page. Migrations triggered by a rebrand or acquisition are covered in our article on [rebrands, acquisitions and site migrations](/blog/dental-practice-rebrand-seo-migration/). If rankings were already weak before the redesign, see [why dental websites do not rank](/blog/why-dental-websites-do-not-rank/) and our [dental SEO service](/services/dental-seo-company/), since a redesign rarely fixes a relevance or local-signal problem on its own.

## Maintainability and choosing a CMS

The platform matters less than whether the practice can keep the site accurate after launch. Hours change, dentists join and leave, insurance participation shifts, and offers expire. A site that needs a developer for each of these drifts out of date.

We choose a platform with the practice by asking who edits, how often, what has to integrate (booking, forms, reviews, tracking), who applies security and software updates, and what happens if the relationship with any vendor ends. The practice should own the domain, the hosting account or have a clear transfer right, and hold admin access. The detailed comparison is in [WordPress versus Next.js for dental websites](/blog/wordpress-vs-nextjs-dental-websites/).

## What the practice needs to provide

- **Decisions and approvals** from one person with authority, within agreed review windows.
- **Facts:** insurance participation, fees or fee ranges you are willing to publish, policies, hours and team details.
- **Clinical review** of every clinical statement by one of your dentists.
- **Access** to the domain registrar, hosting, current CMS, Search Console, analytics and the booking system vendor.
- **Front-desk readiness:** someone who answers calls and responds to form requests promptly. A better site produces more requests; an unanswered queue wastes them.
- **Photography** of the real practice and team where possible; stock photos of strangers in scrubs tell visitors nothing.

## Options, limitations and budget drivers

We do not publish prices. Cost depends on:

- **Scope:** fix in place, redesign on the same platform, or full rebuild.
- **Page count and content depth:** a handful of core pages versus many treatment and location pages.
- **Integrations:** booking systems, patient forms, financing and review widgets each add testing.
- **Migration size:** the number of URLs to map and the state of the old site.
- **Accessibility debt:** retrofitting a site built without accessibility in mind takes longer than building it in.
- **Number of locations:** groups need location templates and governance, which overlaps with our [multi-location SEO service](/services/local-seo-multi-location-clinics/).

Limits we state up front: third-party booking and chat tools are only as fast and accessible as their vendors make them; a website cannot fix an unanswered phone; and no design change can promise a particular ranking or booking outcome.

## A worked example

<Illustrative title="A two-dentist practice with a mobile drop-off">
A two-dentist general practice gets most of its website visits on phones. Its analytics show plenty of visits to the new-patient page but few form submissions, and the front desk reports that some patients say they "couldn't find a time".

Task testing finds three problems. The booking widget loads after several seconds and opens in a small embedded frame that is hard to scroll on a phone. A sticky chat button covers the form's submit button on smaller screens. And the insurance page lists twelve plans, three of which the practice left last year.

The practice's platform is editable and its URLs are clean, so the decision is to fix in place. The changes: open booking in a full-screen view with a request form as a fallback for visit types the widget cannot handle; move the chat button and confirm the submit button is never obscured; update the insurance list with the billing lead; and add a short "what happens at your first visit" section. Calls, form submissions and completed online bookings are tracked as separate actions.

Over the next quarter, the practice compares completed bookings from the website, and the share of those bookings that attend, against the previous quarter, taking account of a hygienist's leave that reduced capacity for three weeks. All details in this example are invented for illustration.
</Illustrative>

## How success is measured

We separate four levels for every website engagement:

- **Inquiries:** calls from the site, form submissions and online booking completions, each tracked as its own action.
- **Qualified inquiries:** inquiries from people the practice can serve, for the services it wants.
- **Bookings:** appointments with a date in the schedule.
- **Attended visits:** patients who arrived.

We also report diagnostic measures such as task completion in testing, Core Web Vitals at the 75th percentile and accessibility issues resolved, but we do not present these as business results. Definitions follow our [dental marketing metrics glossary](/resources/dental-marketing-metrics/). Comparisons are made against the practice's own baseline, with changes in capacity, seasonality and ad spend noted.
