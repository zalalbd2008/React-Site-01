## Why nearby patients may not see your practice, and where we would start

A practice can have a good website and still be missing from the map results that appear for "dentist" searches near its office. The map pack runs on different signals from the main organic results. The Business Profile is the main input, the searcher's location matters a great deal, and small factual errors — a wrong category, hours that disagree with the website, a second profile for the same address — can quietly suppress a listing.

Our recommendation for a single office is to work in this order: **eligibility and accuracy first, then relevance, then prominence.** A profile that breaks a guideline is at risk of suspension, and no amount of review activity compensates for that. Once the profile is safe and correct, the most useful work is making it and the website describe the practice specifically. Reviews and local reputation come last in the sequence, not because they matter less but because they take longest and depend on everything else being right.

If your main question is why the practice does not show in Maps at all, our article on [Google Maps visibility for dentists](/blog/google-maps-visibility-for-dentists/) covers the diagnosis in depth. Dental groups with several offices need a different approach, set out under [multi-location dental SEO](/services/local-seo-multi-location-clinics/).

## What Google publishes about local ranking

Google's Business Profile Help states that local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and popularity, and that there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. The page's section on the third factor is headed "Prominence". It keeps the algorithm's details confidential, so the weights are unknown. The table below sets out what the page says and what it means for a dental office.

| Factor | What Google says | What a practice can influence | What it cannot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relevance | How well a profile matches what someone is searching for; complete, detailed business information helps Google understand the business | Accurate categories, services, description and a website page that matches the profile | Searches for treatments the practice does not offer |
| Distance | How far the business is from the searcher, or from the location Google infers | Nothing directly, beyond keeping the address precise | Where patients are when they search |
| Prominence (summarised by Google as popularity) | How well known the business is, including information such as links to the business and the number of reviews; more reviews and positive ratings can help | Genuine reviews, legitimate local mentions and links, consistent information across the web | How quickly reputation accumulates |

The same help page lists practical steps: verify the business, keep information complete (address, hours, category, attributes), respond to reviews and add photos. None of these are secret; the difference lies in doing them accurately and consistently.

## How we diagnose a dental Business Profile

### Is the profile eligible and represented the way Google requires?

Google's representation guidelines say a profile should reflect the business as it is consistently recognised in the real world — signage, stationery and branding — with a precise address and only one profile per business location. A rented mailing address or virtual office is not eligible, and an office in a co-working space qualifies only with clear signage, staffing and customers received during business hours. Businesses showing an address should keep permanent fixed signage at that address.

Name rules are strict. Taglines, service descriptions, location information, phone numbers and fully capitalised words are listed among the things a business name must not include. For a dental office, the common problems are names extended with "Implants and Cosmetic Dentistry" or a neighbourhood that does not appear on the sign.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

### Are practitioner and practice profiles set up correctly?

Google lists dentists among individual practitioners. A dentist may have a dedicated profile when public-facing and reachable at the verified location during stated hours; support staff should not, and one dentist should not hold several profiles for different specialisations. Where several practitioners share a location, the practice keeps its own profile and practitioner profiles carry only the dentist's name. A solo dentist in a branded practice is advised to share one profile named "brand: practitioner name".

### Is the business information accurate and consistent?

We compare the profile's address, phone, hours and website link against the practice's own website, signage and the main directories. Google's guidelines ask for a phone number that connects to the individual location and is under the business's direct control, a local number rather than a central call-centre line where possible, and a website link that represents that location. Regular customer-facing hours should be listed, with special hours for holidays. If the practice uses a call-tracking number on the profile, we check that it meets these conditions before recommending it.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

### Do the categories describe what the practice is?

Google asks businesses to use as few categories as possible, choose the most specific ones that represent the main business, and pick categories that complete the statement "this business IS a" rather than "this business HAS a". Categories are not for keywords or attributes. For a dental office this usually means a precise primary category for the type of practice and additional categories only where the practice truly operates as that type of business. Services the practice offers belong in the services list, the description and the website.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

### Is the review process inside the rules?

For local ranking, the point is narrow: Google counts reviews toward prominence, and its Maps content policy allows businesses to ask for genuine reviews but not to offer incentives or ask only satisfied patients. We check the current request process against that policy as part of the profile diagnosis. The full rule set (Google, the FTC rule and patient privacy in replies) and the ongoing request-and-reply routine belong to [dental reputation management](/services/dental-reputation-management/); the mechanics of asking are in [a compliant dental review request process](/blog/dental-review-request-process/).

<SourceNote ids={["google-maps-ugc-prohibited-content", "google-gbp-local-ranking"]} />

### Does the website back up the profile?

The profile's website link should land on a page that confirms what the profile says (name, address, phone, hours, services at that office, parking and access, approved insurance or payment information) and offers a clear way to book. For a single office that is often the homepage. Google's local business structured data documentation recommends describing each location with the most specific LocalBusiness subtype; we mark up only facts that are visible on the page.

<SourceNote ids={["google-local-business-structured-data"]} />

We avoid the pattern of many near-identical suburb pages pointing at one office. Google's spam policies describe pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page as doorway abuse.

## Responsibilities on the practice side

The facts on a Business Profile are the practice's facts, so most of what keeps it accurate happens inside the office.

- **Ownership.** The practice should own the Business Profile. We work as a manager, and access can be removed at any time.
- **Accurate information.** Someone must tell us promptly about changes to hours, holidays, phone systems, dentists joining or leaving, and any move.
- **Real-world consistency.** Signage, letterhead and the website must use the same practice name the profile uses.
- **Photos.** Genuine photos of the office, team and signage, supplied by the practice with any necessary permissions.
- **Review requests.** Front-desk staff ask every patient in the same way, without incentives or screening, and never post reviews themselves.
- **Phone handling.** A call from the map listing is an inquiry happening now; if it goes to voicemail at lunch, the visibility gain is lost.
- **Privacy.** Review replies never confirm that someone is a patient; see [responding to negative dental reviews](/blog/responding-to-negative-dental-reviews/).

## Scope choices, limits and what changes the cost

| Situation | Likely scope | Main constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Clean profile, practice wants upkeep | Monitoring, review routine, quarterly location-page updates | Gains are incremental; prominence builds slowly |
| Profile with errors or duplicates | One-off clean-up then light monitoring | Some fixes depend on Google's review of edits |
| Suspended or unverified profile | Evidence preparation and reinstatement request first | Outcome depends on Google's decision |
| New office opening | Profile creation timed with signage and opening, plus location page | Cannot verify before the office is genuinely operating |

The main cost drivers are the amount of clean-up, how many practitioner profiles exist, whether the location page needs building from scratch, the volume of review replies the practice wants handled, and whether call and form tracking is in place. A single-office practice that also needs its broader website to rank for treatments should scope that under [dental SEO services](/services/dental-seo-company/) rather than expect local SEO to cover it.

A practice two miles from a dense residential area will generally appear for different searchers than one in an office park, whatever the profile says, because distance is one of the three factors Google names. Local SEO also cannot make an incentivised or filtered review programme safe, or turn a co-working desk into an eligible address.

## A worked example: a family practice with mixed signals

<Illustrative title="One office, two dentists, a name problem and inconsistent hours">
A family practice with two dentists has one practice profile and two practitioner profiles — one for a dentist who left a year ago. The practice name on the profile includes "Family and Cosmetic Dentistry" plus a neighbourhood name; the sign outside shows only the practice name. Profile hours say the office opens on Saturdays, but the website says weekdays only. Reviews arrive irregularly because staff ask only patients who seem happy.

**Diagnosis.** The extended name conflicts with the naming guideline. The departed dentist's profile still sends calls to the office and confuses patients. The hours conflict risks patients arriving to a closed office and erodes trust in the listing. The review practice amounts to selective solicitation, which Google's policy prohibits.

**Proposed order.** Correct the name to match the sign. Resolve the old practitioner profile with the practice and that dentist. Agree the true hours and update the profile, website and directories together. Replace the ad-hoc approach with a single request method used for every patient at checkout. Build a location page that confirms address, hours, parking, the two dentists and services offered at that office.

**What the report would show.** Suppose the profile recorded 120 calls and 60 website clicks in a month before the changes. The report would show those profile actions, then how many calls the practice's tracking counted as new-patient inquiries, how many of those were qualified, how many booked, and how many attended. Numbers are invented to show the method.
</Illustrative>

## Measuring local results without overstating them

Profile interactions such as calls, direction requests and website clicks are useful signals but they are not patients. Some calls are existing patients rescheduling; some direction requests come from couriers. Local reporting therefore has two layers that are specific to this service, followed by the outcome stages every channel shares:

- **Map visibility**, sampled at fixed points across the service area on a schedule. Sampling shows patterns, not exact positions, because results vary by searcher and device.
- **Profile actions**: calls, website clicks and direction requests from the profile's own performance data, read as signals rather than results.
- **Outcomes**: tracked inquiries from the profile and location page, then how many the practice confirms, books and sees, as totals only.
