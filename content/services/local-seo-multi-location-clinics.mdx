## The problem with SEO across many dental offices

For a group, most local search problems are not ranking puzzles. They are records that disagree with each other: a Business Profile that still shows a departed dentist's name, an acquired office whose old website outranks its new location page, a phone number that routes to a central call centre for one office and directly to the front desk for another, two profiles for the same address because a store code was changed in a spreadsheet. Each of these is small. Across ten or forty offices, they compound into a pattern where some locations are hard to find and nobody can say why.

Our recommendation is to treat multi-location dental SEO as three linked jobs, in this order:

1. **Govern the data.** One authoritative location register, one ownership model for every profile and property, and a rule for who may change what.
2. **Structure the web presence.** A site architecture and location page template that can absorb new offices, closures and rebrands without breaking.
3. **Improve and report office by office.** Visibility work prioritised by each office's capacity and market, measured in a way that compares offices fairly.

If your question is broader, such as how a DSO should split budget across brands, channels and regions, start with our page on [marketing for dental groups and DSOs](/who-we-help/dental-groups-dsos/). This page covers the specific SEO service.

## How we diagnose a dental group's local search position

The diagnosis starts by building a picture of what exists, not by running a ranking report. Rankings are an output; the inputs are the records Google reads.

### The location register comes first

We ask for, or help assemble, a single list of every office with the fields below. Where the group already keeps a register in its operations or finance systems, we reconcile against it rather than replacing it.

<Checklist title="Fields every office needs in the location register" items={[
  "Public trading name exactly as on signage, and the legal entity behind it",
  "Street address with suite number, as the office presents it to patients",
  "Primary phone number that reaches that office, and any tracking numbers in use",
  "Regular hours, holiday hours and any services with different hours",
  "Booking system and the URL or number a new patient should use",
  "Dentists and hygienists who see patients there, with start and end dates",
  "Services offered at that office, and services it does not offer",
  "Status: open, opening, relocating, closing, recently acquired or rebranding",
  "Google Business Profile ID and store code, website location URL, and owner of each"
]} />

### Then we audit access and ownership

For each office we record who is the primary owner of the Business Profile, who manages it, who controls the domain and DNS, who owns the analytics and Search Console properties, and whether any former agency or departed employee still holds access. A group that has grown by acquisition can inherit profiles owned by a previous owner's personal Google account, which becomes a problem the day that person stops answering email.

### Then we reconcile what Google and the web show

We compare the register with each office's Business Profile, location page and main listings, and log every difference as a defect with an owner: a wrong suite number, a duplicate profile, a category that differs from sibling offices, a page naming a dentist who has left. We also check that location pages are indexed and linked from navigation, and that old acquired-practice URLs redirect somewhere sensible.

### Finally we read the measurement

Before proposing targets, we establish what each office can count today and whether any of it can be matched to new-patient bookings. Where the answer is "not yet", fixing that joins the plan alongside [conversion tracking and attribution](/services/dental-conversion-tracking/).

## Location governance: who can change what

A group needs explicit rules for who can edit which records. Google recommends business groups as a safer alternative to shared logins, and anyone added as a manager or owner of a business group can access all current and future profiles in it.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-business-groups"]} />

Google fixes the roles: a business group can have several owners but one primary owner, and only owners can manage users, delete or transfer the group. Group managers can add, edit and delete every profile but cannot manage users; on a single profile, managers cannot add or remove users or remove the profile.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-business-group-roles", "google-gbp-owners-managers"]} />

So "who can edit" depends on which group a profile sits in and which role a person holds. One group for the whole organisation means one dashboard and spreadsheet, but group access reaches every office; one group per brand or region limits access at the cost of separate spreadsheets. People can also be added to single profiles within a group.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-business-groups"]} />

The model we usually propose is a primary owner who is a group employee, central marketing as owners, the agency as managers, and office managers with profile-level manager access only where they genuinely need to post updates or reply to reviews. The table makes the split concrete.

| Record | Who approves a change | Who makes the change | Who is told |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public name, brand or category | Group marketing lead | Agency, in the business group | Office manager |
| Address, relocation, closure | Operations lead | Agency, after the move date is confirmed | Every channel owner |
| Hours and holiday hours | Office manager | Office manager or agency | Central marketing |
| Phone numbers and call routing | Operations lead | Agency and phone vendor together | Office manager |
| Dentists listed at an office | Clinical or HR lead | Agency | Office manager |
| Review replies | Office manager or central team, per policy | Whoever the policy names | Central marketing |
| Location page content | Group marketing lead | Agency | Office manager |

## Group website structure for many offices

For many dental groups, one domain with a subfolder per office is the simpler choice, because authority, templates and maintenance are shared. Separate domains make sense mainly when offices trade under genuinely different brands with separate booking and patient-facing identities. Subdomains sit between the two and are usually chosen for technical rather than search reasons.

## Business Profiles at scale

### Naming, categories and practitioners

Google's chain guidelines require the same name across locations (unless the real-world name genuinely varies) and the same category for locations providing the same service, with one profile per location. For a group, the practitioner rules matter mostly when associates move between offices: a profile left behind at the old office becomes a stale listing. The rules themselves are covered in our article on [practice and practitioner profiles](/blog/dentist-practice-google-business-profiles/).

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

### Phone numbers and websites per office

Google asks for a phone number that connects to the individual location, or a website that represents it, and prefers a local number over a central call-centre helpline where possible. For groups with central scheduling, this is a real tradeoff: the call centre may answer more reliably, but the profile should still point to something that represents that office. We work through it with operations rather than treating it as an SEO setting.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

### Bulk verification and spreadsheet management

Groups with ten or more profiles of the same business can apply for bulk verification in Business Profile Manager. Google's conditions on the retrieval date include: duplicate, suspended or disabled profiles do not count toward the ten; the spreadsheet must contain every profile the account manages; service-area businesses are excluded; and agencies holding more than one business in one account cannot bulk verify. Google may also de-verify accounts that do not actively manage their profiles.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-bulk-verification"]} />

Bulk management depends on store codes. Each location needs a unique store code that stays the same, and adding or changing a store code on an existing location through a spreadsheet creates a duplicate location. We set store codes once, record them in the register and never let a spreadsheet edit them.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-store-codes"]} />

## Acquisitions, relocations and rebrands

Change is where a growing group most easily loses visibility. Every change has three parts that have to land together: the profile, the website and the listings.

For the website side we follow Google's site-move guidance, which for a group mostly means mapping each acquired practice's old URLs to the matching group location page and keeping the redirects, generally for at least a year.

<SourceNote ids={["google-site-move-url-changes"]} />

The full sequence is in our article on [keeping visibility through acquisitions, rebrands and migrations](/blog/dental-practice-rebrand-seo-migration/). In the service it becomes a playbook per change type with named owners and dates, and we hold the profile name change until the real-world signage and website reflect the new name, because Google expects names to match how the business is represented in the real world.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

## Listings consistency

Directories outside Google still shape what patients see and what Google can corroborate. We correct the listings each office can actually control from the location register and log what changed; we do not promise that every third-party site will update on request.

## Options, limitations and what drives the budget

We do not publish prices. The scope, and so the cost, is driven by:

- **Number of offices and brands.** Ten offices under one brand is a different job from ten offices across four brands.
- **State of ownership.** Recovering profiles held by former owners or vendors takes time and sometimes Google support cases.
- **Acquisition pace.** A group adding offices every quarter needs ongoing change control, not a one-off clean-up.
- **Website platform.** Whether location pages can be templated, and whether the group's developer or ours implements redirects.
- **Measurement maturity.** Office-level reporting beyond inquiries needs call tracking, form routing and some link to booking data.
- **Content depth.** Distinctive pages for each office need office input and writing time.

There are also limits. Google decides what appears in local results; its help page summarises the factors as relevance, distance and popularity (with prominence as the heading for the third), and distance is outside anyone's control. An office in a dense market with little history may improve steadily without ever becoming the most visible practice in its area. Profiles can be suspended or edited by Google or by the public, and we can only respond quickly, not prevent it.

## A worked example: an acquisition and a rebrand

<Illustrative title="A nine-office group acquiring three practices">
A group operates nine offices under one brand on one domain. It acquires three independent practices. Two will rebrand to the group name in four months; one will keep its own name because of strong local recognition.

The diagnosis finds that one acquired practice's Business Profile is owned by the seller's personal Google account, the group's own nine profiles sit in a business group but two office managers have owner access they do not need, and one existing office has a duplicate profile created when a spreadsheet import changed its store code.

The plan: secure ownership of the seller-held profile before closing; move all twelve profiles into group-owned business groups, one per brand; downgrade the two office managers to profile-level access; resolve the duplicate. For the two rebranding practices, redirects go live the day signage changes, and profile names change only once storefront and website show the new name. The independent brand keeps its own domain and is revisited in a year.

The report separates the twelve offices into three cohorts — established, rebranded and independent — so the rebranded offices are compared with their own pre-change baseline rather than with established offices. All figures and timings in this example are invented for illustration.
</Illustrative>

## Reporting: how success is defined and compared

Success is defined per office, using the same inquiry, qualified-inquiry, booking and attended-visit stages for every office so that one office's "lead" means the same as another's. The definitions are in the [dental marketing metrics glossary](/resources/dental-marketing-metrics/); what matters for a group is that every office reports them identically. Many groups can count inquiries per office straight away and the later stages only once call tracking and practice-management data are connected; where a stage is not visible for an office, the report says so rather than estimating it.

Comparing offices fairly is the hard part. A raw league table rewards old offices in quiet markets and punishes new ones in busy ones.
