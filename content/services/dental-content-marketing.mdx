## The content problem a practice usually has

A practice website rarely suffers from too little content. More often it has the wrong content: dozens of short blog posts written to a monthly quota, treatment pages that describe a procedure without saying who it suits or what the consultation involves, an insurance page nobody has updated, and location or service pages that read the same as every other practice's. Visitors leave without the answer they came for. Search engines find little that distinguishes the practice.

Our recommendation is to fix and fill before publishing more. First improve the pages prospective patients already reach and the decisions they are trying to make; then fill the genuine gaps the research shows; and only then build a steady editorial calendar. Content is useful marketing when it helps someone decide whether your practice is right for them, and when it is accurate enough that your dentists are comfortable putting the practice's name to it.

## What Google says about content like this

Google's guidance on content is unusually direct, and it suits dental practices well.

**People-first, not search engine-first.** Google's helpful content guidance asks whether content is created primarily for people rather than to manipulate rankings, and lists warning signs of search engine-first content, including extensive automation across many topics, mainly summarising what others say without adding value, and entering topic areas without real expertise.

<SourceNote ids={["google-helpful-content"]} />

**Health topics get more scrutiny.** The same guidance explains E-E-A-T (experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness) and says Google's systems give even more weight to content aligned with strong E-E-A-T on topics that could significantly affect health. Google adds that E-E-A-T is not itself a specific ranking factor.

<SourceNote ids={["google-helpful-content"]} />

**Who, How and Why.** Google suggests asking who created the content, how, and why. It encourages accurate bylines where readers would expect them, describes fabricated creator profiles and false credentials as deception, and suggests disclosing how automation was used where someone might reasonably ask how the content was made.

<SourceNote ids={["google-helpful-content"]} />

**AI is allowed; scaled filler is not.** Google says it rewards high-quality content however it is produced and that appropriate use of AI is not against its guidelines, but that using automation primarily to manipulate rankings violates its spam policies. Its current generative AI guidance says AI can help with research and structure, warns that generating many pages without adding value may violate the scaled content abuse policy, and advises fact-checking and reviewing AI-generated content before publishing.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ai-content-guidance", "google-gen-ai-content", "google-spam-policies"]} />

For a practice, these points add up to a workable rule: publish fewer, better pages that reflect what your practice actually does, reviewed by the people qualified to review them, under honest names.

## How we diagnose your content

### An inventory of what exists

We list every indexable page and post on the site, with its search impressions and clicks from Search Console, internal links pointing to it, external links where known, the last date it was meaningfully updated, and which service or patient question it serves. The inventory shows whether several posts compete for the same question while none answers it well.

### Research into the questions patients ask

We gather candidate questions from the practice itself (front-desk staff are the richest source: what do callers ask before booking?), from Search Console queries the site already appears for, from the "People also ask" style questions visible in search results, and from the services the practice most wants to grow. Keyword ideas are treated as research candidates, not targets with promised volumes.

### Matching questions to page types

Some questions belong on a treatment page, some on the new-patient or insurance page, some in an FAQ, and a few justify an article of their own. Matching them correctly prevents ten thin posts circling one topic.

### Triage

Each existing page gets one of five decisions.

| Decision | When it applies | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Keep | Accurate, useful, distinct, still earning visits or links | Light checks only; schedule a review date |
| Improve | The right topic but thin, dated or missing the patient's real question | Rewrite or expand; send any clinical changes for dentist review |
| Merge | Several pages competing for the same question | Combine into one stronger page; redirect the others to it |
| Retire | Inaccurate, irrelevant, or about services no longer offered | Remove after checking links; redirect where a close equivalent exists |
| Create | A real question with no adequate page | Add to the editorial plan |

## Clinical review stays with your dentists

This is the most important boundary in the service. Alpha Dental Media is a marketing agency. We research, structure and draft; we check claims against the practice's own information, readability and search intent. We are not dentists, and we do not provide clinical review or clinical advice.

Every statement about diagnosis, treatment options, suitability, risks, recovery or outcomes is reviewed and approved by a dentist at your practice before it is published. Where a dentist is named as reviewer or author on the page, that is because they did the review or the writing. If no dentist has capacity to review a draft, the draft waits.

We may use AI tools for parts of research and drafting, such as grouping questions or producing a first structure. We do not use automation to mass-produce pages, city-by-service variations or near-duplicate posts. ## Distribution: getting approved content in front of people

Publishing a page is not the end of the job. Each approved piece can be adapted for channels the practice already controls: a newsletter or recall email to existing patients, a Business Profile post linking to an updated service page, a short social post, or a printed FAQ for the front desk. Content that answers common pre-booking questions is also useful to the phone team, which helps consistency between what callers hear and what the website says.

We adapt content for distribution; we do not buy links or place articles on networks built to manipulate rankings. Earning genuine local mentions, such as community involvement or local partnerships, is valuable but depends on what the practice actually does.

## Dependencies and what the practice must commit

- **Dentist review time.** This is often the limiting factor. A plan that assumes more drafts than your dentists can review simply produces a queue.
- **Facts only the practice has:** the fee information it is prepared to publish, insurance participation, services offered at each location, and policies.
- **Access** to the website CMS and Search Console, or a developer who can publish changes.
- **A single approver** for voice, topics and non-clinical content.
- **Capacity** to handle the inquiries content produces, especially for services you are trying to grow.

## What sets the scope of a content programme

We do not publish prices. What drives the scope:

- **Size and state of the existing site.** A site with many thin posts needs more triage and redirect work.
- **Number of services and locations.** Each priority service and each office needs distinct content; groups should also see our guide to [writing location pages without duplication](/blog/dental-group-location-pages/).
- **Depth of research.** Interviews with front-desk staff and dentists produce better material and take time.
- **Review cycles.** More rounds of clinical review add time but are not optional.
- **Distribution.** Adapting content for email and social adds work per piece.

Limitations: content does not fix technical problems that stop pages being crawled or indexed, a weak Business Profile, or a booking path that fails on a phone. If those are present, our [dental SEO service](/services/dental-seo-company/) or [website and conversion service](/services/dental-web-design-conversion/) comes first, and [why dental websites do not rank](/blog/why-dental-websites-do-not-rank/) explains how to tell. Content also cannot create demand for a service people in your area are not looking for.

## A worked example

<Illustrative title="An implant-focused practice with sixty thin posts">
A practice wants more implant consultations. Its site has sixty blog posts, most around 300 words, written over several years, plus a single implant page that describes the procedure in two paragraphs and has no information on consultations, cost factors or financing.

The inventory shows that eleven posts are about implants, several cover the same question with slightly different titles, two mention a technology the practice no longer uses, and the implant page appears in search for implant-related queries but few visitors go on to contact the practice.

The plan: retire the two inaccurate posts with redirects; merge the overlapping implant posts into the main implant page and two new pages answering distinct questions the front desk hears often (what happens at an implant consultation, and what affects the cost of treatment); and keep the posts that are accurate and distinct. The practice's lead dentist reviews every clinical statement across the three pages over two review sessions. Remaining posts are triaged over later cycles.

The practice then tracks, from the implant pages, calls and consultation requests separately; how many of those were people it could treat; how many booked a consultation; and how many attended. The practice, its posts and every number here are invented.
</Illustrative>

## How we report content performance

Traffic is the easiest number to report and the least useful on its own. Content is reported by page group (each priority service and its supporting pages) so that the implant pages, say, can be followed from impressions to the calls, forms and consultation requests they produce, and then to the bookings and attended visits the practice can confirm. The stage definitions are the shared ones in the [metrics glossary](/resources/dental-marketing-metrics/); the content-specific part is attributing each inquiry to the page it started from.

Alongside those, we report diagnostic signals that show whether content is being found and used: search impressions and clicks for the improved pages, the queries they appear for, and engagement on key pages. These help decide what to improve next; they are not presented as outcomes. For a sense of when changes typically become visible, see [how long dental SEO takes](/blog/how-long-dental-seo-takes/).
