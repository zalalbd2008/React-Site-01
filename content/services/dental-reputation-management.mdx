## Common review shortcuts, and what we recommend instead

Reviews matter to a practice because prospective patients can read them when comparing dentists, and because Google says the number of reviews and positive ratings can help a profile's local ranking. That pressure leads practices toward shortcuts — a gift card for a five-star review, a survey that sends only happy patients to Google, a staff target of reviews per week, or a sharp public reply correcting a patient's account of their visit. Each of these now carries a policy or legal risk, and the last one carries a patient privacy risk.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-local-ranking"]} />

Our recommendation is a boring system run consistently: ask every patient after their visit through the same channel, offer nothing in return, reply to reviews with short templates that never confirm the person is a patient, and treat negative reviews as service data first and a marketing problem second. The volume of reviews grows from the number of patients asked, not from cleverness.

Profile setup, categories and the map pack belong to our [local SEO and Google Business Profile service](/services/dental-local-seo-google-business-profile/); this page covers the reviews themselves.

## The rules that shape dental review work

### Google's review policies

Google's Maps content policy requires that reviews reflect a genuine experience. It prohibits offering incentives — payment, discounts, free goods or services — in exchange for posting any review, or for revising or removing a negative one. It also prohibits discouraging or prohibiting negative reviews and selectively soliciting positive reviews. Merchants should not require or pressure customers to review while on the premises, should not request specific content, and should not ask staff to solicit a certain number of reviews or reviews that mention a staff member. Content based on a conflict of interest, such as current or former employment or family relationships, is treated as rating manipulation.

<SourceNote ids={["google-maps-ugc-prohibited-content", "google-gbp-review-tips"]} />

Two consequences for dental practices are easy to miss. First, a "review us" incentive aimed at patients is prohibited even if it does not ask for five stars. Second, a front-desk quota or a bonus tied to a number of reviews conflicts with the policy on staff solicitation, even if every review is genuine.

### The FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials

The FTC's Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465, was published on August 22, 2024 and took effect on October 21, 2024. Its provisions most relevant to a dental practice:

- **Fake or false reviews and testimonials** — writing, creating or buying reviews that misrepresent that the reviewer exists, had an experience with the business, or what that experience was (§ 465.2).
- **Buying reviews of a particular sentiment** — compensation or other incentives in exchange for, or conditioned on, reviews expressing a particular sentiment, positive or negative (§ 465.4).
- **Insider reviews** — officers or managers reviewing their own business without clearly disclosing the relationship, and soliciting reviews from relatives or staff that then appear without disclosure (§ 465.5).
- **Company-controlled review sites** that claim to be independent (§ 465.6).
- **Review suppression** — unfounded legal threats, intimidation or knowingly false public accusations aimed at preventing or removing a review, and implying that reviews displayed on your own site represent most or all reviews when negative ones are suppressed because of their rating (§ 465.7).

<SourceNote ids={["ecfr-16-cfr-465", "fr-2024-ftc-consumer-reviews-rule"]} />

Note the difference in scope: the FTC rule targets incentives tied to sentiment, while Google prohibits incentives for any review on its platform. We design to the stricter of the two.

### Text-message requests and the TCPA

If review requests go out as automated texts, federal telemarketing rules also apply. The FCC's rule at 47 CFR 64.1200 requires the called party's prior express consent for many autodialed calls and texts to mobile numbers, and prior express written consent where the message includes advertising or is telemarketing, with separate provisions for health care messages. Which category a review request falls into is a question for the practice's counsel; we use the consent and opt-out process counsel approves and do not switch on automated texting before that. The practical steps are in [requesting dental reviews without incentives or gating](/blog/dental-review-request-process/).

<SourceNote ids={["ecfr-47-cfr-64-1200"]} />

### Patient privacy when replying

A review is the patient's own disclosure; a reply is the practice's. HIPAA's covered entities include health care providers that transmit health information electronically in connection with covered transactions, which can include a practice that submits insurance claims electronically, and covered entities are limited in what they can say about patients in public. As summarised by the law firm DLA Piper, a December 2022 HHS Office for Civil Rights settlement concerned a dental practice found to have impermissibly disclosed patient names, treatment and insurance information in replies to Yelp reviews, including details the reviewers had not posted themselves. The firm's conclusion: unlike patients, covered entities face significant limits when responding to reviews, good or bad.

<SourceNote ids={["ecfr-45-cfr-160-103", "dla-piper-hipaa-review-settlement-2023"]} />

So we adapt one piece of Google's general advice. Google suggests addressing reviewers by name and acknowledging their specific feedback; for a practice, naming the reviewer or referring to "your visit", "your treatment" or the clinical details can confirm a patient relationship. Our templates thank people, describe general policies and invite contact with the office, without confirming that anyone is a patient. Google agrees on the core point: never share a reviewer's private information, and take complex issues offline. The full method is in [responding to negative dental reviews without disclosing patient information](/blog/responding-to-negative-dental-reviews/).

## What the service delivers

- **A request routine.** One message, sent to every patient after an appointment through the practice's existing patient communication system or a review tool, with a direct review link. Google supports review links and QR codes for this. No incentives, no satisfaction filter, no requested content.
- **Reply templates and an approval rule.** Short templates for positive, mixed and negative reviews, written to avoid confirming a patient relationship, plus a rule for which replies a named manager must approve before posting.
- **A reply routine.** Replies are posted publicly as the business, Google reviews them against its content policies, and the reviewer is notified and can still change their review. We draft; the practice approves.
- **Monitoring.** Notifications and a weekly check for each office's Google profile and any other platforms that matter locally.
- **Policy flagging.** Reviews that appear to break Google's policies — spam, conflicts of interest, content unrelated to a real experience — are documented and flagged. Google decides the outcome.
- **Service-recovery routing.** Negative themes go to the practice manager with a suggested owner, so the review becomes an operational fix rather than only a reply.
- **A clean-up of past replies** where earlier responses disclosed patient details, prioritised with the practice and its counsel.

## A worked example: replacing a gated survey

<Illustrative title="A three-office group with an uneven review profile">
A three-office group uses a survey tool that asks patients to rate their visit from 1 to 5. Patients who choose 4 or 5 see a link to Google; patients who choose 1 to 3 see a "tell us more" form that goes to the office manager. Office A has a high rating and many recent reviews; Office C has few reviews and two unanswered complaints about billing from the last quarter. Front-desk staff receive a small bonus for each Google review mentioning their name.

**Diagnosis.** The survey routing is selective solicitation, which Google prohibits. The staff bonus is a quota tied to reviews that name staff, which Google's policy also addresses. Two past replies at Office B mention the reviewer's treatment.

**Changes.** The survey is rebuilt so every respondent sees the same Google review link, whatever score they give, and the private feedback form remains available to all. The staff bonus is replaced with a measure the practice controls: the share of completed appointments that received a request. The two disclosing replies are edited to a general template after counsel review. Office C's billing complaints go to the group's billing lead, and both receive a reply inviting the reviewer to contact the office.

**Three months later.** Office A's rating dips slightly as some less satisfied patients now reach Google; Office C's review count rises because requests are now sent there consistently. The group reports request coverage, reply time and billing-related themes alongside rating and volume, so the dip at Office A is read as a more representative profile rather than a failure.
</Illustrative>

## Reporting and how success is defined

Reputation reporting should connect to patient acquisition without pretending reviews alone produce patients. We report:

- **Process measures** the practice controls: share of completed appointments sent a request, reply time, share of replies using approved templates, and zero replies containing patient details.
- **Profile measures**: review volume and recency by office, rating trend, and themes by month.
- **Acquisition context**, from the [conversion tracking service](/services/dental-conversion-tracking/) where it is in place: what happened to inquiries from the Business Profile and organic search further down the path to an attended visit, never attributed to reviews alone.
