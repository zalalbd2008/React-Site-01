## Why dental ad accounts waste money, and what we recommend first

Most dental Google Ads problems are not caused by the ads themselves. They come from four quieter decisions: who the account is allowed to reach, which searches it pays for, where those visitors land, and what it counts as success. When those are loose, the account can report a healthy number of conversions while the front desk sees few new patients.

Our direct recommendation for most practices is to fix measurement and targeting before adding budget. More spend on a poorly defined account buys more of the same problem. If the practice cannot currently tell an ad call from a recall call, or a booked implant consultation from a pricing enquiry, the first month of work goes into that, because every later decision depends on it.

Google Ads is also not the right channel for every practice at every moment. If the schedule is already full for the treatments you would advertise, or calls regularly go unanswered at lunch, ads will mostly generate frustrated callers. We say so in the audit rather than starting a campaign that the practice cannot serve.

## How we diagnose an existing account

The diagnosis follows the money from search to visit. We look at what the account is set up to do, what it actually paid for, and what happened after the click.

### Who can see the ads: location settings and schedule

Google offers two inclusion options. "Presence or Interest", which Google describes as the default and recommended option, reaches people who are in or regularly in the targeted area and also people who have shown interest in it. "Presence" limits ads to people likely to be located, or regularly located, in the targeted area. Google's case for the broader option cites its internal data from travel, real estate and education advertisers, not dentistry.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-advanced-location-options"]} />

For a practice whose patients must physically attend, we usually test "Presence" first, then compare against the broader setting only where there is a reason to reach people researching the area from elsewhere — for example a destination implant or full-arch practice that draws from a wide region. We also check radius and ZIP-level targeting against where existing patients actually come from (in aggregate, never patient-level lists), and whether ads run during hours nobody can answer the phone.

### What the budget paid for: search terms and match types

Keywords are not the same as searches. Google states that broad match can show ads on searches related to a keyword, including searches that do not contain the keyword's direct meaning, and recommends using Smart Bidding with it. Phrase match shows ads on searches that include the keyword's meaning; exact match on searches with the same meaning or intent. The search terms report shows the actual searches, but only those used by a significant number of people; low-volume terms are omitted for privacy and may be grouped as "other queries".

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-match-types", "google-ads-search-terms-report"]} />

So we review the search terms report, estimate how much spend sits in terms it does not show, and classify what it does show: treatment intent, emergency intent, job seekers, dental school and course searches, DIY and home remedies, other practices' names, and patients searching for services you do not offer. Our article on [choosing keyword match types for dental ads](/blog/dental-google-ads-keyword-match-types/) covers the tradeoffs in detail.

### What happened after the click: landing pages and phones

We open every final URL on a phone. Common findings are ads sending implant searches to the homepage, landing pages without a visible phone number, booking widgets that fail on mobile, and forms that never send a confirmation. Ad call data shows whether calls were connected and how long they lasted; we compare that with the practice's call handling so that missed calls are treated as a staffing or routing problem, not a keyword problem.

### What the account calls a conversion

We list every conversion action and whether it is primary (used for bidding and reporting) or secondary (observed only). Accounts often have page views, button clicks, every call of any length and duplicate form tags set as primary. Smart Bidding then optimises toward whatever is easiest to produce, which is rarely a new patient.

## What the management service delivers

- **A structure that mirrors how patients search.** Separate campaigns or ad groups for the treatments you want to grow — for example general new-patient care, emergency appointments, implants, clear aligners — with brand campaigns kept apart so they do not inflate the totals for new-patient campaigns.
- **Geographic and schedule settings tied to capacity.** Location option, areas and ad schedule set to where patients can realistically travel from and when the phones are staffed. Emergency campaigns are scheduled around real same-day availability; our guide to [running emergency dental ads around hours and capacity](/blog/emergency-dental-advertising/) explains why.
- **Keywords, match types and negatives.** Research candidates for each treatment, match types chosen for the level of control the data supports, and negative keyword lists maintained from the search terms report. Google notes that negative keywords do not match close variants, so synonyms and singular or plural forms need adding separately. The method is set out in [building dental negative keyword lists](/blog/dental-google-ads-negative-keywords/).
- **Ads and assets.** Responsive search ads that state the facts patients compare — insurance accepted, location, appointment availability — plus call assets scheduled to show only when someone can answer, and location assets where Business Profiles are linked.
- **Landing pages.** One page per major treatment intent, with a visible phone number, a working booking or request path and the information a patient needs to choose. We can brief or build these, or coordinate with your [website and conversion work](/services/dental-web-design-conversion/).
- **Conversion definitions and offline outcomes.** A short list of primary conversions that mean a real inquiry, and, where the practice can support it, imported outcomes such as booked or attended appointments. That work is part of our [conversion tracking and attribution service](/services/dental-conversion-tracking/).
- **Monthly review and change log.** A written record of what changed and why, so you can judge decisions rather than just results.

## Healthcare ad policies that shape a dental account

Dental advertising is ordinary local advertising in most respects, but two Google policy areas affect it directly.

**Healthcare and medicines.** Google restricts the use of prescription drug terms in ads, landing pages and keywords, and does not allow the promotion of speculative or experimental medical treatments. A practice that names prescription products in its advertising or on landing pages — some sedation or facial aesthetic offerings, for instance — should expect those terms to be reviewed under this policy and may see ads limited or disapproved. We check landing pages for this before launch rather than after a disapproval.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-healthcare-policy"]} />

**Personalized advertising.** Google lists health among its sensitive interest categories. Advertisers promoting products and services in sensitive categories cannot use advertiser-curated audiences, which include Customer Match, website data segments (formerly remarketing) and lookalike segments; predefined Google audiences remain available. Whether a given dental ad falls in the health category is Google's decision, but we plan dental accounts on the assumption that remarketing and patient-list targeting will not be available, and we do not recommend uploading patient lists without advice from the practice's counsel.

## Options, limitations and budget drivers

We do not publish prices. Management scope is agreed after the audit, and the ad budget is a separate decision that sits with the practice. What moves the management effort is predictable:

| Driver | Why it changes the work |
|---|---|
| Number of locations | Each office needs its own areas, schedule, call routing and reporting split |
| Number of treatment campaigns | Implants, aligners, emergency and general care each need keywords, ads, negatives and pages |
| Landing page work | Briefing, building or testing pages adds effort outside the ad account |
| Measurement depth | Importing booked or attended outcomes requires practice-system work and staff process |
| Competition in the area | Crowded markets need more frequent search terms and bid review |
| Policy-sensitive services | Sedation, facial aesthetics or financing claims need extra review |

For setting the ad budget itself, see [how to set a dental ad budget from qualified inquiries and attended visits](/blog/dental-google-ads-budget/) and model scenarios with the [dental growth calculator](/resources/dental-growth-calculator/).

## A worked example: an implant campaign that looked successful

<Illustrative title="A two-dentist practice advertising implant consultations">
A two-dentist general practice spends a fixed monthly amount on one campaign that mixes general, emergency and implant keywords. The account reports 60 conversions a month. The practice manager estimates about eight new implant consultations a month from all sources and cannot say how many came from ads.

**Diagnosis.** The conversion list shows "all calls" set as primary with no minimum duration, plus a button-click action on every page. Of the 60 conversions, roughly 35 are clicks on the phone number in the website header, many from existing patients. Location is set to "Presence or Interest" with a 40-mile radius. The search terms report includes searches for dental assistant jobs, implant courses and another practice's name.

**Changes.** Implants move to their own campaign with a dedicated landing page stating consultation steps and financing options approved by the practice. Primary conversions become calls of at least a set duration from ads, completed consultation request forms and online bookings; header clicks become secondary. Location switches to "Presence" with the radius narrowed to the area where patients actually come from. Negative lists cover jobs, education and other practices' names.

**What the report shows next quarter.** Reported conversions fall to 22 a month — which looks worse. But the front desk now marks implant consultations booked from ad calls and forms: 9 booked, 6 attended in the first month after the change. The practice can now compare ad cost to attended consultations instead of to an inflated conversion count, and decide whether to raise or cut the budget.
</Illustrative>

## How we report and define success

Reports separate four stages, because each answers a different question and has a different owner.

Google's documentation describes measuring calls by minimum duration or, with call recording on, by AI analysis of recordings in the US and Canada; recording patient calls raises consent and privacy questions that the practice's counsel should review before it is switched on. Where the practice can export booked or attended outcomes, Google Ads supports offline conversion imports so that bidding can learn from them. Metric definitions live in the [dental marketing metrics glossary](/resources/dental-marketing-metrics/), and [measuring calls without counting every call as a new patient](/blog/dental-call-tracking/) explains call classification.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-call-reporting", "google-ads-measure-calls", "google-ads-offline-conversions"]} />

A successful quarter is defined in advance: for example, a lower cost per qualified inquiry for implant searches, a higher share of ad calls answered, or a set number of attended consultations within capacity. We do not count success by impressions, clicks or reported conversions alone.
