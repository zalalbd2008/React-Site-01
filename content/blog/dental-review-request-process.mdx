<KeyAnswer>Ask every eligible patient for a review through the same channel at the same point after their visit, send the same public review link to all of them, and offer nothing in return. Do not filter requests by satisfaction, set staff quotas, pressure patients on the premises or ask for specific content. Confirm texting consent with counsel before automating SMS.</KeyAnswer>

This page covers how to *ask* for reviews. How to *reply* to them, especially critical ones, without disclosing patient information is a separate question, answered in [responding to negative dental reviews](/blog/responding-to-negative-dental-reviews/).

## The rules that shape a dental review request process

Two rule sets apply to almost every US dental practice: Google's policies for reviews on Maps and Business Profiles, and the Federal Trade Commission's rule on consumer reviews. Texting adds a third, the TCPA. They overlap but are not the same, and the stricter one usually sets the practical limit.

### What Google allows and prohibits

Google's Maps user-generated content policy says reviews must reflect a genuine experience. It prohibits merchants from:

- offering incentives "such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services" in exchange for posting any review, or for revising or removing a negative one;
- discouraging or prohibiting negative reviews, or "selectively solicit[ing] positive reviews from customers";
- requiring or pressuring users to leave ratings or reviews "while on the premises";
- requesting that staff solicit a certain number of reviews;
- requesting reviews that include specific content, "including content that identifies a staff member".

It does allow merchants to solicit reviews that represent a genuine experience "without offering incentives to do so or attempting to influence the rating or the contents of the review". The policy also treats reviews from employees, family members and others with a conflict of interest as rating manipulation.

<SourceNote ids={["google-maps-ugc-prohibited-content"]} />

Google's Business Profile help adds the mechanics: practices can ask customers to use a Google review link or QR code, and Google encourages valuing all reviews, noting that a mix of positive and negative feedback often feels more trustworthy.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-review-tips"]} />

### What the FTC rule prohibits

The FTC's Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465, took effect on 21 October 2024. Several sections bear directly on a dental office:

- **465.4, buying reviews.** A business may not provide compensation or other incentives "conditioned expressly or by implication on" reviews expressing a particular sentiment, positive or negative.
- **465.5, insider reviews.** An officer or manager may not write a review of the business without clearly disclosing the relationship, and may not solicit reviews from employees or immediate relatives without instructing them to disclose it. Generalised requests to all customers are carved out.
- **465.7, review suppression.** Unfounded legal threats, intimidation or knowingly false public accusations aimed at preventing or removing a review are prohibited, as is implying that reviews displayed on the business's own site represent most or all reviews when negative ones are being suppressed.

### How the two compare

| Practice | Google policy | FTC rule (16 CFR Part 465) |
|---|---|---|
| Any incentive for any review, even neutral | Prohibited | Not addressed directly; 465.4 targets sentiment-conditioned incentives |
| Incentive only for positive reviews | Prohibited | Prohibited (465.4) |
| Asking only happy patients (gating) | Prohibited as selective solicitation | Not a named violation on third-party sites; on the practice's own site, hiding negative reviews while implying completeness is prohibited (465.7(b)) |
| Staff or family reviewing the practice | Prohibited as conflict of interest | Restricted without clear disclosure (465.5) |
| Staff review quotas | Prohibited | Not addressed |
| Threatening reviewers to remove reviews | Not the focus of this policy | Prohibited where unfounded or knowingly false (465.7(a)) |
| Asking every patient the same way | Allowed | Allowed (generalised solicitations) |

<SourceNote ids={["google-maps-ugc-prohibited-content", "ftc-consumer-reviews-rule"]} />

The practical rule follows: design to Google's stricter standard, and the FTC's requirements are largely met along the way. Practices with questions about a specific programme, such as a referral reward or a patient-appreciation event that happens near review requests, should ask counsel, because timing and wording can make an otherwise lawful offer look conditioned on a review.

## What review gating looks like in a dental office

Gating is rarely announced as gating. It usually arrives as a software feature or a well-meant habit:

- A post-visit survey asks "How was your visit, 1 to 5?" and only 4s and 5s are shown the Google link, while 1s to 3s see a "tell us privately" form.
- The front desk is told to hand review cards only to patients who "seemed happy".
- Requests go out only after hygiene recalls, never after emergency or treatment visits where outcomes are less predictable.
- An automated flow pauses requests for patients with an open balance or a recent complaint.

Some of these have operational logic. A practice may reasonably choose not to send automated messages to a patient in an active billing dispute. The test is whether the rule is based on the patient's predicted sentiment. A neutral rule applied to everyone (for example, "no requests to minors' own phones" or "no requests within 24 hours of a sedation appointment") is defensible; a rule designed to keep unhappy patients away from the public link is not.

The honest alternative is simple: show every eligible patient both options, the public review link and a private feedback route, in the same message.

## Designing the request: timing, channel and wording

### Timing

Ask once, soon after the visit, while it is fresh, and optionally remind once. A sensible default for most general practices is a message the same afternoon or the next day, and a single reminder a few days later if the link has not been opened. More than that becomes nagging and generates opt-outs.

Decide which visits trigger a request and apply that rule to everyone: for example, every completed appointment for patients 18 and older, at most once every six months per patient. Record the rule in writing so it can be shown to be neutral.

### Wording

A good request is short, neutral and identical for everyone. It names the practice, thanks the patient for visiting, asks for honest feedback, gives the public link, and offers a private contact for concerns. It does not mention a specific procedure, ask for five stars, suggest what to write or name a staff member to praise.

<Callout title="An example request message" tone="example">
"Thank you for visiting Example Family Dental. If you have a minute, we would value an honest review of your experience: [review link]. If something did not go as you expected, you can also reach our office manager directly at [phone or email]. Reply STOP to opt out of texts."
</Callout>

Note what it avoids: no "5-star", no "Dr. X would love a mention", no "your crown appointment", no offer.

## Texting consent: where the TCPA comes in

Automated text messages to mobile numbers fall under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act and the FCC's rules at 47 CFR 64.1200. In broad terms, those rules require the called party's prior express consent for many autodialed calls and texts to mobile numbers, and prior express *written* consent for those that include advertising or constitute telemarketing. The rules contain specific provisions for health care messages from HIPAA covered entities.

Whether a review request is informational, a health care message or marketing is not settled by the rule text alone, and it is exactly the kind of question for the practice's counsel. A cautious practice collects clear written consent to receive texts from the practice, including feedback requests, at intake.

Revocation rules matter as much as consent. Under 64.1200(a)(10), a patient may revoke consent by any reasonable method, and replies such as "stop", "quit", "end", "revoke", "opt out", "cancel" or "unsubscribe" count as revocation. A further FCC rule, published 26 October 2026 and effective 25 November 2026, lets callers treat a revocation as applying only to the category of informational messages it was directed at and to designate an exclusive revocation method. A practice using one texting platform for reminders and review requests should check with its vendor and counsel how opt-outs are scoped after that date.

<SourceNote ids={["ecfr-47-cfr-64-1200", "federal-register-fcc-revocation-2026"]} />

Privacy is a separate layer. Under the HIPAA Privacy Rule a covered entity may not use or disclose protected health information except as the rule permits. Keep review messages free of appointment types, procedures or clinical details, and check with the practice's privacy officer how the review platform vendor handles patient contact data.

## The staff workflow

The process should not depend on anyone's judgement about who deserves a request.

Measure the process, not people. Track requests sent as a share of eligible visits, opt-out rate and reviews received per hundred requests at practice level. Do not rank front-desk staff by reviews collected, which Google's policy on staff quotas rules out and which tends to bring back informal gating.

<Illustrative title="A practice audits its own process">
A three-chair practice finds that 61 review requests went out last month against 240 completed adult appointments, a request rate of about 25%. The cause: requests were sent manually, only when the front desk remembered, and mostly after hygiene visits. The office manager switches to an automated trigger on every completed adult appointment with a one-per-six-months cap. The following month the request rate is about 90%. Average rating dips slightly as more treatment patients are asked, then stabilises. The practice treats the dip as a more honest picture, not a problem to engineer away.
</Illustrative>

## Common mistakes and tradeoffs

- **Using the vendor's default 'smart' flow.** Some review platforms offer satisfaction-first flows. Check whether low scores change which link appears; if they do, switch it off.
- **Asking only after 'easy' visits.** Hygiene-only requests can produce flattering averages and thin, repetitive reviews. Asking after all visit types is fairer and gives prospective patients more useful detail.
- **Over-messaging.** Multiple reminders inflate opt-outs and can cost the practice the ability to text appointment reminders, which matter more.
- **Rewarding staff indirectly.** A "review of the month" bonus is a quota by another name.
- **Treating review volume as the goal.** Reviews support local visibility and conversion, but the outcome that matters is attended visits. How reviews fit into Maps visibility is covered in [why a practice may not show in Google Maps](/blog/google-maps-visibility-for-dentists/).
