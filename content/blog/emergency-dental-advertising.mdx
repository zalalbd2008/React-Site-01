<KeyAnswer>Run emergency dental ads only during hours when someone answers the phone and same-day slots really exist. Set the campaign ad schedule and call asset schedule to those hours, keep Business Profile hours accurate, target the distance a patient in pain will travel, and make the landing page say exactly what is available. Measure answered calls and attended visits, not clicks.</KeyAnswer>

Emergency searches are among the most time-sensitive a practice can buy. The searcher usually wants to talk to someone now and be seen soon, which makes the ad's value depend almost entirely on operations: phone coverage, open slots and the team's willingness to fit someone in. This article covers how to align a campaign with those constraints. Measuring calls without counting every ring as a new patient has its own article: [dental call tracking](/blog/dental-call-tracking/).

## Start with a capacity definition, not a campaign

Before any setting is changed, the practice needs to write down what "emergency availability" means in its own schedule. The marketing team cannot infer it, and the answer usually differs by day of the week.

<Checklist title="Emergency capacity questions to answer before advertising" items={[
  "How many emergency slots are held open each day, and on which days",
  "When held slots are released to regular patients if no emergency calls",
  "Who answers the phone at each hour, including lunch and the last hour of the day",
  "Whether there is an after-hours on-call arrangement, and what it can actually offer",
  "Which emergencies the clinical team will and will not see, decided by the dentists, not the marketing team",
  "Whether new patients are accepted as emergencies, or only existing patients",
  "What payment or insurance information callers are told up front",
  "How the front desk records that a booked visit came from an emergency call",
  "What happens on days when a dentist is out and capacity drops"
]} />

The clinical scope question matters for the landing page as well as the schedule. What the practice says about swelling, trauma or when to go to a hospital is a clinical decision for the dentists to write or approve. Marketing should present that content accurately, not draft it.

## Matching the campaign to the hours

Google Ads gives a practice three settings that control when an emergency ad can reach a caller, and they need to agree with each other.

**The campaign ad schedule.** Google states that campaigns are set to show ads "All day" by default. An ad schedule can restrict days and hours, or apply bid adjustments to particular times. When a schedule is used, Google says the campaign paces toward a monthly spend of 30.4 times the average daily budget regardless of how many days it is active, so a schedule concentrates spend into fewer hours rather than reducing the monthly total.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-ad-scheduling"]} />

**The call asset schedule.** Google lets advertisers set phone numbers to appear only when the business can accept calls, and under advanced options to schedule the call asset for certain days and hours. Call clicks cost the same as headline clicks. If the campaign runs at hours when the call asset is off, the ad can still appear, just without the call button, which suits a page that offers online booking requests overnight.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-call-assets"]} />

**Location assets and Business Profile hours.** Google says location asset data, including hours, comes from Google Business Profile and Maps, and that locations marked temporarily or permanently closed will not show as location assets. The Business Profile guidelines ask for regular customer-facing hours and allow special hours for holidays and other events. Inaccurate profile hours therefore show up inside the ads too.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-location-assets", "google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

The "open, slots full" band is the hardest. An ad schedule knows the clock, not the appointment book. A practice whose emergency slots are often gone by 11 a.m. either needs a front-desk habit of signalling when they fill, or an ad message that does not promise same day.

## Targeting the distance a patient will travel

Emergency intent is local. A patient in pain is less likely to drive across a metro area than one shopping for aligners, so emergency campaigns usually justify tighter geography than other campaigns.

Google offers two targeting bases: people in or regularly in the locations you target ("presence"), and people who have shown interest in those locations. The default advanced option uses both. Google recommends "Presence or Interest" for Search in general, but its own help page says Presence targeting makes sense when you only want people in specific locations rather than people elsewhere who are interested in them. An emergency campaign for one office is a reasonable case for Presence.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-location-targeting", "google-ads-target-locations"]} />

Google also warns that a very small radius or location may serve only intermittently or not at all, because small targets may not meet its targeting criteria. Tight is good; tiny can mean no delivery. Check the campaign's geographic report after a few weeks to see where clicks and calls actually came from.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-target-locations"]} />

## Writing a landing page that keeps the ad's promise

Google's Misrepresentation policy includes "Unavailable offers": promising products, services or offers in the ad that are unavailable or not easily found from the destination is not allowed. For emergency dentistry, the obvious risk is an ad that says same-day or 24/7 when the practice cannot deliver it.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-misrepresentation"]} />

Beyond policy, an honest emergency page tends to be shorter and more practical than a general service page:

- **The phone number first, and the hours it is answered**, in text, not only in a button.
- **What same-day means here**: for example, "we hold emergency appointments each weekday and fill them in order of calls", if that is true.
- **What happens after hours**, stated plainly, including whether anyone answers.
- **Who the practice sees**: new patients, existing patients, children, and any insurance limits.
- **What to bring and what it may cost**, to the extent the practice is willing to say.
- **Clinical guidance only if the dentists wrote or approved it.**

Page speed and the mobile call path matter more here than on almost any other page; the website side is covered by the [dental web design and conversion service](/services/dental-web-design-conversion/).

## Measuring emergency campaigns

Google can count a call from an ad as a conversion in two ways: by counting calls longer than a minimum duration the advertiser sets, or by using Google's AI to analyse call recordings for lead quality, where recording is available and enabled. Call reporting uses Google forwarding numbers. Whether recording calls to a dental practice is appropriate is a privacy and legal question the practice should decide with its own counsel before turning it on.

## Tradeoffs and common mistakes

Running emergency ads only when capacity exists means fewer impressions and accepting that some emergency demand goes to other practices. Running them all day buys more calls, but some of them reach voicemail or a full schedule, which costs money and can produce frustrated reviews. There is no setting that removes the tradeoff; the choice should follow the practice's capacity.

Common mistakes:

- Leaving the campaign at the default all-day schedule.
- Scheduling the campaign but not the call asset, or the reverse.
- Business Profile hours that differ from the website and the ads, especially around holidays.
- Promising same-day or 24/7 on the ad or page when it is not consistently true.
- Excluding "emergency" as a negative keyword everywhere instead of controlling it by schedule; see [dental negative keywords](/blog/dental-google-ads-negative-keywords/).
- Counting every call from an ad as an emergency patient.
