<KeyAnswer>Dental SEO cost depends on scope, not on a market rate: how many locations and services you need to appear for, the state of the website, how much new content is required, how competitive the local area is, and how results are reported. Compare proposals by converting each into a list of named deliverables, ownership terms and measures, then compare price against that list.</KeyAnswer>

Practice owners often receive SEO proposals that look impossible to compare. One quotes a monthly retainer with a vague list of "optimisations", another a project fee for a technical clean-up, a third an hourly rate. This article explains what drives the effort behind those numbers, what a proposal should spell out, and a method for putting different proposals on the same footing. It does not give price ranges. We have not found a published pricing survey with a documented dental sample, date and method that would justify quoting one, and a figure without those details tends to anchor the conversation in the wrong place.

## Why published price ranges are a weak guide

Price ranges for "SEO" circulate widely. Before relying on any of them, check four things: who published it, when, how many respondents it surveyed and whether any of them were working with dental practices. Most figures fail at least one test. Even a well-run survey of agencies measures what agencies charge across many industries, not what a particular practice needs.

The more useful benchmark is Google's own advice on hiring an SEO. Its Search Central page "Do you need an SEO?" suggests interviewing the provider, asking what results they expect and in what timeframe, checking references, and treating an audit as a source of "realistic estimates of improvement, and an estimate of the work involved". It also warns against providers who promise first place in results. Google's guide to how Search works states that Google does not accept payment to rank a site higher, and Business Profile Help says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. So any fee is paying for work, not for access to Google.

## What actually drives the cost of dental SEO

The effort, and therefore the fee, is driven by a small number of factors. Most of them can be assessed before a proposal is written, which is why a proposal produced without any review of your site and profile should be treated with caution.

Two drivers are easy to underestimate. The first is the **website platform**. Some practice-website products restrict who can edit templates, redirects or page structure; if the SEO provider cannot change them, they either work around the limits or bill the website vendor's time. The second is **measurement**. If the practice cannot tell an inquiry from a booked or attended visit, any SEO report will default to rankings and traffic, and you will be paying for activity you cannot evaluate. The difference between those stages is set out in our [dental marketing metrics guide](/resources/dental-marketing-metrics/).

## What a dental SEO proposal should itemise

A proposal you can compare names the work, the output, the owner and the measure. Use the list below to check whether each proposal you receive is complete.

<Checklist title="A complete dental SEO proposal states" items={[
  "What the provider reviewed before writing the proposal, and what it found",
  "Technical work in the first phase: indexing, redirects, duplicate pages, mobile issues",
  "Which treatment and location pages will be created or rewritten, and how many",
  "Who writes content, who checks clinical accuracy, and how the dentist approves it",
  "Business Profile work: categories, services, photos, posts, review process, and whether it is included in the fee",
  "How local references and links will be earned, with no paid links that pass ranking credit",
  "What the monthly report contains, and whether it separates inquiries from bookings",
  "Who owns the website, content, Search Console, Analytics and Business Profile accounts",
  "Term, notice period and the exit process, including how access is handed back",
  "What the practice must provide, by when, and what happens if it is late",
  "Any separate fees: set-up, content, tools, link outreach, profile management"
]} />

Google's spam policies treat buying links that pass ranking credit as link spam. If a proposal includes a set number of links per month without saying where they come from, ask. Earned local references, such as community organisations, professional associations and genuine sponsorships, take time and practice involvement, and a proposal should say who does that work.

## Business Profile fees and ownership

Google publishes rules for agencies that manage Business Profiles for clients. They are useful to know when reading any proposal that includes profile management.

- **Ownership stays with the practice.** The third-party policies say businesses decide whether a partner has co-owner, manager or no access, and that end customers must keep ownership or co-ownership of their profile at all times.
- **Fees must be disclosed.** A provider that charges a management fee must tell the business that Business Profile itself is provided at no extra cost, inform new customers in writing before managing the profile, and show the fee on invoices.
- **Leaving must be easy.** Within 7 business days of notice, the provider must let the business regain exclusive control of the profile.
- **Reports must separate data.** Business Profile data must be reported separately from data from other platforms.
- **No unrealistic claims.** Among the false or unrealistic claims the policies give as examples is a promise of top placement on Google.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-third-party-policies"]} />

None of this means a management fee is wrong. Profile work takes time: categories, services, photos, responding to reviews, monitoring edits that other users suggest. It means the fee should be visible and the practice should never need the provider's permission to access its own listing.

## How to put different proposals on the same footing

Proposals are rarely structured alike. The method below converts each into the same shape so that price becomes the last thing you compare, not the first.

1. **List the deliverables.** For each proposal, write down every named piece of work with a quantity: pages rewritten, pages created, technical fixes, profile tasks, reports.
2. **Strike out the vague items.** "Ongoing optimisation", "monthly SEO activities" and "keyword tracking" without detail count as nothing until the provider defines them.
3. **Add the hidden work.** Note anything the practice must supply, any separate fees and any tools billed on top.
4. **Mark ownership and exit.** For each proposal, note who owns the accounts and content, the term and the exit process.
5. **Mark the measures.** Note what each report would show and whether it reaches booked or attended visits.
6. **Only then compare price** against the remaining, defined deliverables.

Once converted, A cannot be compared at all until the provider defines its work and changes the account arrangement. B and C are comparable: B costs more in total if content is included, but C shifts writing time onto the practice manager. ## Tradeoffs worth deciding before you choose

**Retainer or project.** A retainer suits ongoing content and local work; a project suits a defined technical problem such as a migration. Many practices need a project first and a smaller retainer after, and a proposal that offers only one model may be fitting the practice to its billing rather than the other way round.

**Agency writes or practice writes.** Practice-written content is cheaper and can be more authentic, but it often stalls. Agency-written content costs more and needs the dentist's time for accuracy checks. Decide who has capacity before comparing fees.

**Narrow or broad scope.** Concentrating on two or three priority treatments and the core location is easier to measure than spreading effort across every service. Broad scope can look like more value and deliver less.

**Speed versus risk.** Faster ranking promises usually lean on tactics such as bought links or many near-identical city pages, both of which Google's spam policies address. Slower plans built on accurate profiles, useful pages and earned references are less exposed to policy changes.

## Common mistakes when comparing SEO proposals

- **Comparing headline fees.** Two proposals with similar prices can buy very different work.
- **Accepting rankings as the only measure.** Rankings for chosen keywords can rise while calls stay flat. Ask for reports that reach inquiries and, where the practice can supply them, booked visits.
- **Letting the provider own the accounts.** Profiles, Search Console and Analytics should sit in the practice's own accounts, with access granted to the provider. Google's "Do you need an SEO?" page suggests granting only read access to Search Console at the audit stage.
- **Ignoring the practice's share of the work.** Approvals, photos, interviews and front-desk call handling all affect results. A proposal that says nothing about them is incomplete.
- **Expecting quick results to justify the cost.** Google's SEO Starter Guide notes some changes take several months to show. Judge early months on whether the defined work was done, and see [how long dental SEO takes](/blog/how-long-dental-seo-takes/) for what to measure first.
