<KeyAnswer>Visitors usually do not book because the path from interest to action breaks on a phone: the call button is hard to find or hit, the form asks too much or fails silently, the booking tool does not fit new patients, insurance questions go unanswered, or the page is slow or unusable with assistive technology. Walk that path yourself, on a phone, step by step.</KeyAnswer>

This article explains why each of those breaks matters and how to find them. The [dental website audit checklist](/resources/dental-website-audit-checklist/) is the working tool to tick off during the audit; this page explains the reasoning behind its items so you can judge what to fix first. If the problem is that too few people reach the site at all, that is a visibility question covered in [why dental websites do not rank](/blog/why-dental-websites-do-not-rank/).

## Where a mobile visit to a dental website stalls

A prospective patient on a phone typically wants to answer three questions before acting: can this practice help with my problem, can I afford it or use my insurance here, and how do I get an appointment. Each answer has to be findable within a thumb's reach. When any one of them is missing or awkward, most people do not email to ask; they press back and try the next result.

It helps to separate the visit into stages, because each stage fails for different reasons and has different owners.

The last stage is not technically part of the website, but it is where many "website problems" actually live. A form that submits correctly into an inbox nobody checks looks, from the patient's side, exactly like a broken form.

## Page speed and stability: what to measure and what to ignore

Google's web.dev guidance defines three Core Web Vitals: Largest Contentful Paint should occur within 2.5 seconds, Interaction to Next Paint should be 200 milliseconds or less, and Cumulative Layout Shift should be 0.1 or less, each assessed at the 75th percentile of page loads and segmented by mobile and desktop.

<SourceNote ids={["webdev-core-web-vitals"]} />

For a dental site, those three metrics map onto recognisable patient frustrations. A slow LCP is usually a large hero photo or video that delays the headline. A poor INP shows up as a tap on "Book now" or a menu that seems to do nothing for a moment, often because chat widgets, review carousels and tracking scripts are competing for the phone's processor. Layout shift is the call button jumping down as a cookie banner or promotional strip loads, so the visitor taps the wrong thing.

INP observes the latency of all click, tap and keyboard interactions during a visit and reports the longest, ignoring outliers, which makes it the metric most connected to whether booking controls feel responsive.

<SourceNote ids={["webdev-inp"]} />

web.dev's LCP guidance breaks the metric into server response time, resource load delay, resource load duration and element render delay; diagnosing which part is slow tells you whether the fix is hosting, image handling or scripts.

<SourceNote ids={["webdev-optimize-lcp"]} />

Keep speed in proportion. Google's page experience documentation says Core Web Vitals are used by its ranking systems but that good scores alone will not put pages at the top of results, and that chasing a perfect score for SEO reasons may not be the most useful investment. For conversion, the threshold that matters is "fast and stable enough that nobody notices", measured with field data from real visits rather than a single lab test on office Wi-Fi.

<SourceNote ids={["google-page-experience"]} />

## Tap targets and call buttons

On a phone, the call button is often the most valuable element on the page. It is also where small design decisions do the most damage.

WCAG 2.2 success criterion 2.5.8 asks for pointer targets of at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, with exceptions for adequately spaced targets and inline links; the enhanced criterion 2.5.5 uses 44 by 44 CSS pixels. Phone numbers in headers, text-link "call us" prompts and tightly stacked footer links frequently fall below the minimum.

<SourceNote ids={["w3c-wcag22-target-size-minimum"]} />

Consistency matters as much as size. WCAG 2.2 criterion 3.2.6 asks that help mechanisms repeated across pages, such as a contact phone number or contact link, appear in the same relative order on each page. A patient who found the number in the header of the homepage should find it in the header of the implant page.

<SourceNote ids={["w3c-wcag22-consistent-help"]} />

What to check on the call path:

- **Is the number a working tel: link** on every page, including inside images or banners where it is often plain text?
- **Does the button say what happens** ("Call the front desk") and, outside opening hours, what the caller will reach?
- **Does a sticky call bar cover content** or the cookie banner, or obscure form fields when the keyboard opens?
- **Is the number the one the practice wants called?** Old numbers survive in footers, schema markup and PDFs after a phone system change. Tracking numbers have their own rules, covered in [dental call tracking without treating every call as a new patient](/blog/dental-call-tracking/).
- **Does someone answer?** Call the number during lunch and at five minutes before closing. That is part of the audit.

## Forms that lose patients before submission

Appointment request forms fail in two ways: people abandon them, and people complete them but the submission never arrives. Both are invisible unless you test.

### Why people abandon dental forms

The common causes are too many required fields, fields that ask for things patients do not know yet (insurance group number, preferred hygienist), and validation that rejects reasonable input such as phone numbers with spaces or names with apostrophes. Each field should earn its place: what does the front desk genuinely need to call the person back?

WCAG 2.2 requires labels or instructions when content requires user input, and requires that automatically detected input errors are identified and described to the user in text. A form that turns a field red without saying why fails both the criterion and the patient.

<SourceNote ids={["w3c-wcag22-labels-or-instructions", "w3c-wcag22-error-identification"]} />

Criterion 1.3.5 asks that fields collecting information about the user expose their purpose programmatically, typically through autocomplete values, so browsers can offer to fill name, email and phone. On a phone that turns a minute of typing into two taps.

<SourceNote ids={["w3c-wcag22-identify-input-purpose"]} />

### Why completed forms never reach anyone

Submit a test form and follow it all the way. Does it arrive? In which inbox? Does anyone own that inbox on Saturday? Does the confirmation message tell the person what happens next and when? Is there an automatic reply that sets expectations, and does it avoid repeating sensitive details from the form? A form routed to a former employee's address is one of the most common and most expensive website faults, because the site appears to be working.

Forms also carry privacy obligations. Ask counsel how HIPAA applies to your form provider and to any analytics or advertising tags on pages where patients submit information, and keep clinical detail out of marketing forms where you can.

## Booking flows and the new-patient gap

Online booking tools can be excellent for existing patients and confusing for new ones. Common breaks include booking widgets that load slowly inside a frame, require an account before showing availability, show only hygiene appointments, or offer slots the practice does not actually want new patients to take. Whether a practice should offer self-scheduling at all, or a request form, depends on schedule control and integration; that decision is covered in [online booking versus request forms](/blog/dental-online-booking-vs-request-forms/). For the audit, the question is simpler: can a new patient with a specific problem get from the service page to a confirmed appointment or request on a phone, without dead ends?

## Insurance and cost information that answers the real question

For many visitors, "do you take my insurance?" is the deciding question, and a page that does not answer it hands the decision to a practice that does. Useful insurance content is specific and dated: the plans the practice is in network with by recognisable names, a plain statement of what happens if a plan is not listed (for example, the team checks out-of-network benefits), and when the list was last updated. For fee-for-service or elective treatments, explain how cost is determined and what the consultation estimate includes, without quoting prices the practice will not honour.

## Accessibility as a conversion issue

The Department of Justice's web guidance says Title III of the ADA covers businesses open to the public, lists medical offices among its examples, and notes that DOJ has no regulation setting detailed web standards for businesses but considers WCAG helpful guidance.

<SourceNote ids={["doj-ada-web-guidance"]} />

Treat accessibility as part of the conversion audit, not a separate compliance project. Images of text that screen readers cannot read, booking widgets that trap keyboard focus, low-contrast buttons and video without captions all stop real patients from booking. A practical target is WCAG 2.2 AA; ask counsel about specific legal obligations.

## Worked example: a twenty-minute phone walkthrough

<Illustrative title="An office manager audits a three-dentist general practice website on her own phone">
She opens the site from a Google search on mobile data, not office Wi-Fi. The homepage headline appears after a noticeable pause while a video loads, and the call button in the header shifts down when a promotional banner appears; her first tap opens the banner instead.

On the emergency page, the phone number is in an image, so tapping it does nothing. The "Request appointment" form has nine required fields, including insurance member ID. With an apostrophe in a test surname the form shows a red outline and no message. On the second try it submits and shows "Thank you" with no indication of when the practice will call.

She checks the shared inbox: the test arrived, but the inbox is checked once a day. The insurance page lists two plans the practice left a year ago. The booking button opens a widget that offers only recall hygiene appointments.

Seven findings in twenty minutes; five are fixed within a week without a redesign. The point of the example is the method, not the specific faults, which are invented.
</Illustrative>

## Common mistakes when auditing conversion

- **Testing on desktop or on office Wi-Fi.** Patients are on phones and mobile networks.
- **Stopping at the thank-you page.** Follow the submission and the call to a human.
- **Redesigning before fixing.** Many conversion faults are configuration, not design.
- **Counting every form or call as a lead.** Existing patients, vendors and spam inflate the numbers; [tracking setup and attribution](/services/dental-conversion-tracking/) separates them.
- **Fixing everything at once.** Change the call path first, measure, then move on, so you know which fix mattered.
