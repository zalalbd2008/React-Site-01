<KeyAnswer>A dental practice should have one profile per office. A dentist may have their own profile only if they are a public-facing practitioner who can be reached at that verified location during stated hours. In multi-dentist offices the practice and each eligible dentist get separate profiles; a solo dentist at a branded practice should share one profile named "Practice: Dr. Name".</KeyAnswer>

The question sounds administrative, but it decides where patients land, which profile collects reviews and calls, and whether Google treats your listings as legitimate or as duplicates. This article covers the profile structure itself. Why a correctly structured profile still does not rank in the map pack is a separate question, covered in [why a practice may not show in Google Maps](/blog/google-maps-visibility-for-dentists/).

## What Google means by an individual practitioner

Google's Business Profile guidelines define an individual practitioner as "a public-facing professional, typically with their own customer base", and name dentists explicitly alongside doctors and lawyers. A practitioner's profile may include a title or degree certification; Google's examples are Dr., MD, JD, Esq. and CFA. DDS and DMD are not listed, but they are degree designations of the same kind.

Google sets two conditions for a practitioner to have a dedicated profile:

1. **They operate in a public-facing role.** Google states that support staff should not create their own profiles.
2. **They can be contacted directly at the verified location during stated hours.**

Two further restrictions matter for dentistry. A practitioner should not have multiple profiles to cover different specializations, so a dentist who places implants and provides orthodontics still has one profile. And the profile is tied to a verified location, which raises questions for dentists who rotate between offices (covered below).

### Where the definition leaves room for judgement

The guidelines do not say whether a hygienist, an orthodontic treatment coordinator or a visiting specialist counts as public-facing. Read conservatively, the test is whether patients seek out and book with that named person, and whether calls to the profile's phone number can reach them during the hours shown. A hygienist whose patients are scheduled by the practice and who is not contacted directly is closer to Google's "support staff" description than to a practitioner. When the answer is unclear, the cautious choice is not to create the profile: an unnecessary profile can be added later, while a duplicate flag or suspension takes time to undo.

The solo-practitioner rule is easy to miss. Google recommends that when a practitioner is the only public-facing practitioner at a location and represents a branded organization, the practitioner shares a single Business Profile with the organization, named using the format `[brand/company]: [practitioner name]`. Google's own example is an insurance agency, but the logic is the same for a single-dentist practice with a brand name. The practical effect is that all reviews, calls and photos accumulate on one profile rather than two that compete for the same searches at the same address.

For multi-dentist offices, Google says the organization "should create a Business Profile for this location, separate from that of the practitioner", and that the practitioner profile "should include only the name of the practitioner". A profile titled "Dr. Ana Ruiz – Riverbend Dental" does not follow that rule. So many dental practitioner listings carry the practice name that it is worth checking each one.

### Departments are not a workaround

Google allows departments within businesses to have their own profiles when they are public-facing and operate as distinct entities, typically with a separate customer entrance, a name that differs from the main business and a different primary category. A general practice that also offers implants in the same operatories is not a department; Google says that if you offer different services you should list them on the profile rather than create separate profiles. A separately signed and separately entered pediatric or orthodontic clinic inside a larger building may meet the department description, but that needs to be true in the real world, with signage, before it is represented on Google.

## Why dental practices end up with duplicate profiles

Google is explicit that there should be one profile per business, and that a profile considered a duplicate will not show on Search or Maps. Google lists three common reasons a profile is marked duplicate: a verified profile already exists for the same business, several profiles share an address, or several profiles exist for the same business offering different services.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-duplicates"]} />

In dental offices, duplicates usually come from ordinary events rather than deliberate manipulation:

- **Relocation handled as a new listing.** Google says a business that moves should update its existing profile with the new address, not create a new one. A fresh profile at the new address leaves the old one, with its reviews, either orphaned or competing.
- **Rebrand or acquisition.** A new owner creates a profile under the new name while the old one still exists, often claimed by a former owner or an old agency account.
- **Practitioner profiles that should have been the practice.** A solo dentist ends up with a practitioner profile and a practice profile at the same address, both verified.
- **Service-specific listings.** Separate profiles for "implants" or "emergency" at the same address. These run directly against Google's instruction to list services on one profile.
- **Profiles created by patients or data providers.** Unclaimed listings appear from user edits and need to be claimed or reported rather than ignored.

Three details in Google's guidance change what you should do first:

- **Removal is destructive.** When you remove a duplicate profile, Google says all content and linked managers are removed. If the duplicate holds most of the practice's reviews, removing it is the wrong move.
- **Merging combines reviews but may lose replies.** Google states that reviews are combined in a merge but replies to reviews may be lost, and that the profiles must represent the same business with the same information. Export or screenshot review replies you care about before requesting a merge.
- **Ownership requests are time-boxed.** If a verified profile is owned by someone else, such as a former partner or a previous agency, you can request access, and the owner has three days to respond. Google notes the claim option is not always available if they do not.

If a profile is already suspended rather than merely duplicated, the evidence you need is different; see [what to prepare before appealing a profile suspension](/blog/dental-google-business-profile-suspension/).

## When an associate dentist leaves the practice

Google's help pages, as retrieved on 8 November 2026, do not describe a procedure for a practitioner who leaves a practice. What follows is reasoning from the rules above, not Google policy.

A practitioner profile describes a person who can be contacted at a verified location during stated hours. Once the dentist no longer practises there, the profile no longer meets that condition at that address. The practice's realistic choices are:

1. **If the dentist is moving to another practice**, the profile arguably belongs with the dentist. Updating it to the new location is a decision for the dentist and whoever owns the profile, and it is easier if ownership was agreed in advance.
2. **If the dentist is retiring or leaving dentistry**, marking the profile closed or requesting removal is the usual route. Expect that some calls and searches for the dentist's name will continue for a while.
3. **If the profile is unclaimed**, the practice can suggest edits on Google Maps, but has limited control over the outcome.

The underlying problem is ownership. Google's role model has one primary owner per profile, with additional owners and managers; only owners can add or remove users or remove the profile. A practice that lets each associate create and own their profile on a personal Google account has no control when that associate leaves. A practice that owns every practitioner profile it created may face a dispute about whether a departing dentist can take "their" profile. Agree in writing, when the dentist joins, who owns the practitioner profile, who are managers, and what happens to it on departure.

## A profile inventory you can run in an afternoon

Before changing anything, list what exists. Searching the practice name, each dentist's name, the address and the main phone number on Google Maps usually surfaces every profile at the address, including ones no one remembers creating.

The link targets matter more than they appear to. A practitioner profile pointing to the practice homepage gives patients searching for a named dentist no information about that dentist. A bio page that states the dentist's role, services and booking route makes the profile useful, and it keeps the practitioner profile distinct from the practice profile.

<Illustrative title="A three-dentist office tidying its listings">
An invented general practice, Harbor Lane Dental, has three dentists at one address. The inventory finds five profiles: the practice; Dr. Lee and Dr. Patel as practitioners; "Harbor Lane Dental Implants", created by a former agency; and Dr. Owens, who left eighteen months ago.

Dr. Owens's profile is still owned by his personal account. The practice contacts him; he has joined another practice and updates the profile to his new address himself.

The practice ends with three legitimate profiles and one merge request pending review. It also has a written rule for future associates about profile ownership.

## Tradeoffs and common mistakes

**Separate dentist profiles are not free visibility.** They can help patients who search for a named dentist, and they let that dentist's reviews describe that dentist. They also divide reviews and calls between profiles at the same address, and each one needs accurate hours, a working phone route and a real bio page. For a practice where patients choose the office rather than the individual, the practice profile carries most of the value.

**Common mistakes:**

- Creating a practitioner profile for a solo dentist alongside the practice profile at the same address.
- Including the practice name, services or city in a multi-dentist office's practitioner profile titles.
- Creating new profiles for a relocation, a rebrand or a new service line instead of editing the existing profile.
- Removing a "duplicate" without checking which profile holds the reviews.
- Letting associates or agencies hold primary ownership of profiles the practice depends on.
- Treating hygienists or treatment coordinators as practitioners without checking whether patients contact them directly.

For groups with many offices and rotating providers, profile structure is part of a wider location architecture; see [how to write useful location pages for dental groups](/blog/dental-group-location-pages/).

## Sources and limitations

The eligibility, naming and department rules come from Google's guidelines for representing a business on Google, and the duplicate, merge, removal and ownership-request behaviour from Google's duplicate-profile help article, both retrieved on 8 November 2026. Those sections apply the general rules and say so. Nothing here predicts how a merge, appeal or removal request will be decided.
