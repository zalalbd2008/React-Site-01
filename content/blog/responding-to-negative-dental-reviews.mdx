<KeyAnswer>Reply briefly and politely, without confirming the reviewer is or was a patient and without any detail about care, appointments, billing or insurance. Acknowledge the concern in general terms, state the practice's standard, and invite the person to contact a named role privately. Flag reviews that break Google's policies through its reporting process, and involve counsel before any legal step.</KeyAnswer>

How to *ask* for reviews fairly is covered in [a review request process without incentives or gating](/blog/dental-review-request-process/). This page is about what to do once a critical review is public.

## Why dental review replies carry a privacy risk other businesses do not have

A restaurant can reply "we're sorry your steak was overcooked on Saturday". A dental practice that writes "we're sorry your crown appointment on Saturday ran late" has publicly confirmed that a specific, identifiable person is its patient and what treatment they had.

Under HIPAA, a health care provider that transmits health information electronically in connection with a covered transaction, such as an electronic insurance claim, is a covered entity; that describes many dental practices, though each should confirm its status with counsel. The Privacy Rule's general standard is that a covered entity may not use or disclose protected health information except as the rule permits or requires. A public reply to a review is not listed among the permitted purposes, which is why enforcement has followed replies that included patient details.

<SourceNote ids={["ecfr-45-cfr-160-103", "ecfr-45-cfr-164-502"]} />

The practical position comes from a 2013 letter by the HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR) to a plastic surgery practice. As quoted by the law firm ArentFox Schiff (we did not open the letter itself), OCR wrote that a covered entity "may not confirm or deny that a particular person was, in fact, a patient", or disclose other identifiable information such as name or address. That is the standard to write to.

<SourceNote ids={["arentfox-ocr-online-reviews-2022"]} />

Law firms and trade publications have reported several OCR actions against dental practices over review replies. Outside dentistry, Nixon Peabody reported a June 2023 OCR settlement with a psychiatric practice whose Google review replies allegedly disclosed information about a patient's diagnosis and treatment; the investigation reportedly found disclosures affecting four patients.

<SourceNote ids={["nixonpeabody-ocr-online-reviews-2023"]} />

Three lessons recur across these reported cases. Replies that *add* detail beyond the review are the core problem. In two of the accounts, investigators reportedly found more than the one reply that prompted the complaint, which suggests a defensive habit repeats. And missing written policies on PHI and social media are reported alongside the disclosures themselves. None of this means every reply is risky; it means the reply process needs rules, not improvisation.

Google's guidance supports this split. It says you can report any review but only those that violate its policies are eligible for removal, and that you should not report a review "just because you disagree with it". ## Writing a reply that does not confirm a patient relationship

### Principles

- **Speak about policy and standards, not the person.** "Our practice aims to…" rather than "When you came in…".
- **Never reference dates, appointments, treatments, providers seen, bills, balances, insurance or outcomes**, even to correct an error in the review.
- **Do not restate the reviewer's claims back to them.** Repeating "your extraction" confirms it.
- **Invite contact with a role, not a demand.** Give the practice's own phone or email. Google's guidelines permit merchants to post their own business contact information in review replies, but not to solicit personal information.
- **Keep it short.** Google's own advice is that long replies lose readers before the main point.
- **Never share the reviewer's private information or attack them.** Google's reply tips say this for every business; for a practice, "private information" includes the fact of being a patient.
- **Sign as the practice or a role**, such as "Practice Manager", rather than a staff member's full name.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-guidelines", "google-gbp-review-tips"]} />

### Phrases to avoid and safer alternatives

| Risky phrasing | Why it is a problem | Safer alternative |
|---|---|---|
| "We're sorry your visit with Dr. Lee didn't meet expectations." | Confirms a visit and a provider | "We're sorry to read that this experience did not meet expectations." |
| "Our records show you missed two appointments." | Discloses patient records | "We take scheduling concerns seriously and would like to understand what happened." |
| "Your insurance didn't cover the procedure, which we explained." | Discloses insurance and treatment | "Questions about costs and coverage are important, and our team is glad to go through them privately." |
| "As we discussed at your last visit…" | Confirms a relationship and a conversation | "We'd welcome the chance to talk this through directly." |
| "This person was never our patient." | Also a statement about a specific person's status | "We can't discuss any individual on a public site, but we'd like to hear from you directly." |

The last row surprises people. Denying that someone is a patient is still a statement about their status, and if the denial is wrong it is worse. The neutral line works whether or not the reviewer was ever a patient.

Use these as patterns, not copy to paste on every review. Google's tips advise against sending the same message to everyone. Vary the opening and the concern acknowledged; keep the privacy line and the contact route constant.

## What happens after you reply

Replies are public and appear as the business. Google checks each reply against its content policies, usually within about 10 minutes but occasionally up to 30 days, and may ask you to edit it. The reviewer is notified and can still change the review afterwards; if they do, the review date updates.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-manage-reviews"]} />

That last point is the most constructive part of the process. A complaint resolved privately sometimes leads the patient to update the review on their own. The practice must not ask for that in exchange for anything. Google prohibits incentives "in exchange for posting any review or revision or removal of a negative review".

<SourceNote ids={["google-maps-ugc-prohibited-content"]} />

## Why legal threats are usually the wrong first move

When a review feels false, the urge is to threaten the reviewer. Two things argue against doing that without counsel. First, the FTC's consumer review rule, at 16 CFR 465.7(a), prohibits using "an unfounded or groundless legal threat", intimidation, or a knowingly or recklessly false public accusation to prevent a review or have it removed. Second, a public reply accusing the reviewer of lying can itself draw attention and, in a healthcare setting, may slide into disclosure.

<SourceNote ids={["ftc-consumer-reviews-rule"]} />

This does not mean a practice has no remedies. Lawyers note that some reviews may be defamatory and that counsel can evaluate options. The order matters: report policy violations to Google, reply neutrally if at all, and let counsel decide whether anything further is justified.

<SourceNote ids={["arentfox-ocr-online-reviews-2022"]} />

<Illustrative title="One review, two replies">
A one-star review says: "Waited 50 minutes, then was told my cleaning wasn't covered and charged anyway. Never again."

**Reply that creates risk:** "Hi Maria, we checked your file. You arrived 20 minutes late for your 2:00 hygiene appointment, and our front desk explained your plan's frequency limit before treatment."

**Privacy-safe reply:** "Thank you for sharing this. We can't discuss anyone's visit or billing here, but long waits and unexpected costs are exactly what we want to hear about and fix. Please contact our practice manager at [office phone] so we can talk directly. — Practice Manager"

The second reply is shorter, addresses what a prospective patient cares about (the practice takes waits and costs seriously) and discloses nothing.

## Common mistakes

- **Replying angry, fast and alone.** The reported dental enforcement examples involved replies that went beyond the review to argue or add facts. A rule that no reply goes up within a few hours of reading the review, and that non-routine replies need a second approver, targets exactly that failure.
- **Flagging every bad review.** Google will not remove reviews for disagreement, and mass flagging wastes the practice manager's time that would be better spent on private follow-up.
- **Letting an agency improvise.** Anyone replying for the practice needs the same written policy as staff.
