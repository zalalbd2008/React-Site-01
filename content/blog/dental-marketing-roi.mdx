<KeyAnswer>Dental marketing ROI is the profit an activity produced, minus its full cost, divided by that cost. To calculate it, separate five numbers that reports often blur: inquiries (leads), booked appointments, attended visits, collected revenue and profit. ROAS, which Google Ads defines as conversion value divided by ad spend, is a useful bidding signal but is not ROI.</KeyAnswer>

Disagreements between practices and agencies about whether marketing "works" are often disagreements about definitions. The agency points at leads; the owner looks at the schedule and the bank account. Both can be accurate and still describe different things. This guide separates the stages, explains why each stage loses volume, and works through a full example from ad spend to profit. If you want this measured properly in your own accounts, that is the job of our [dental conversion tracking service](/services/dental-conversion-tracking/).

## ROAS versus ROI: two different questions

Google Ads defines return on ad spend as conversion value divided by ad spend, multiplied by 100%: in its own example, $5 of sales for each $1 of spend is a 500% ROAS. Conversion value is whatever the advertiser assigns, either the same value for every conversion or a different value per conversion, and Google uses it in bidding strategies such as Target ROAS and Maximize conversion value. Target ROAS needs conversions with values greater than zero, and Google notes that setting a target too high may limit the traffic your ads receive.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-target-roas", "google-ads-conversion-values"]} />

The important word is *assigns*. If a practice tells Google that every tracked call is worth $300, the account will report ROAS based on that number, whether or not those calls became attended, paying patients. ROAS answers "how much reported value did each ad dollar produce?" ROI answers "after every cost, did this make money?"

| | ROAS | ROI |
|---|---|---|
| Formula | Conversion value ÷ ad spend × 100% | (Profit contribution − total marketing cost) ÷ total marketing cost × 100% |
| Cost included | Media spend only | Media, management fees, tracking, creative, landing pages |
| Value included | Whatever value was reported to the platform | Collected revenue after delivery costs, within a stated window |
| Good for | Comparing campaigns and guiding automated bidding | Deciding whether to keep, grow or cut a channel |
| Main risk | Inflated by assumed lead values | Needs data from outside the ad platform |

## A worked example from ad spend to profit

<Illustrative title="One month of paid search for a two-dentist general practice">
**Costs.** Ad spend is $4,000 and the agency management fee is $1,500, so the total marketing cost is $5,500.

**What the ad account reports.** The account recorded 120 conversions (calls and forms). The practice had set a value of $300 for each, so the account shows $36,000 in conversion value and a ROAS of 900% ($36,000 ÷ $4,000 × 100%).

**What the practice records.** Of the 120 conversions, 20 were duplicates, existing patients or spam, leaving 100. Of those, 70 were prospective new patients asking for services the practice offers. 48 booked a first visit and 38 attended.

**What was collected.** Across those 38 patients, the practice collected $15,960 from patients and insurers within 90 days of the first visit, an average of $420 each.

**What it cost to deliver.** The practice's accountant estimates that variable costs of delivering that care (clinical and hygiene time, supplies, lab) run at about 60% of collections, leaving a 40% contribution: $6,384.

**The result.** ROAS on collected revenue is $15,960 ÷ $4,000 = 399%, well below the 900% the platform showed. ROI over 90 days is ($6,384 − $5,500) ÷ $5,500 = about 16%. The campaign covered its full cost in the window, narrowly, before counting any later treatment or recall visits.

**What would change the answer.** If 30 rather than 38 patients had attended, contribution would fall to about $5,040 and the 90-day ROI would be negative. That is why the attendance step, which is mostly an operations question, matters as much as the bidding.

Every number in this example is invented for illustration.
</Illustrative>

## Lifetime value: useful, but easy to misuse

Lifetime value (LTV) estimates what a new patient is worth across future visits and referrals. It is a reasonable idea: a general practice patient who stays for years is worth far more than their first visit. It is also the easiest number to stretch.

Treat LTV with these caveats:

- **It rests on assumptions.** Retention, recall attendance, treatment acceptance and referrals all have to be estimated. If those estimates come from the practice's long-standing patients, they may not hold for patients acquired through a new channel.
- **It arrives slowly.** Value that will be collected in year three does not pay this quarter's ad invoice. Compare it against payback time, not only total return.
- **It should be a range.** Present a low, central and high estimate, and say which assumptions drive the difference.
- **It needs the same cost treatment.** A lifetime revenue figure must be reduced to lifetime contribution before it is compared with acquisition cost.
- **It can justify almost anything.** If a high LTV is used to approve any acquisition cost, nobody will notice when a channel stops working. Keep a short-window measure, such as 90-day collected contribution, alongside it.

## Connecting practice outcomes back to campaigns

Ad platforms and analytics tools only see what happens online unless you send them more. Two mechanics matter.

**Offline conversion imports.** Google Ads lets advertisers import outcomes that happen offline, such as in an office or by phone, so campaigns can be judged on what happened after the click or call. Google currently recommends starting with enhanced conversions for leads, which uses hashed user-provided data alongside click identifiers. Google's help page also notes that since 15 June 2026 these uploads have been migrating from the Google Ads API to the Data Manager API, so check which method any integration uses. For a dental practice, any upload that relates to patients raises privacy questions; involve your own privacy officer or counsel before sending anything derived from patient records, and send the minimum needed.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-offline-conversion-imports"]} />

**Attribution models.** Google Analytics assigns credit for key events using a model, such as data-driven attribution or paid and organic last click. Its documentation notes that direct visits receive no credit unless the whole path was direct. Attribution answers which touchpoints get credit for a recorded action; it does not see phone calls that tracking missed, word-of-mouth referrals or a patient who drove past the sign.

<SourceNote ids={["ga4-attribution"]} />

The practical minimum for most practices is simpler than either: record the source of every new patient at booking, reconcile tracked inquiries with booked and attended visits each month, and keep the definitions fixed. Multi-office groups face the extra problem of comparing offices fairly, covered in [how to build a fair group marketing dashboard](/blog/dental-group-marketing-dashboard/).

## What attribution cannot tell you

Even with clean tracking, return figures describe association, not proof that the marketing caused every visit. Before crediting a channel, check what else changed in the same period:

<Checklist title="Confounders to check before claiming a return" items={[
  "Seasonality, such as school holidays or the end of the insurance benefit year",
  "A new dentist, extended hours or a change in available appointments",
  "Joining or leaving an insurance network",
  "A new office, relocation or rebrand",
  "Changes to tracking itself, such as a new call-tracking number that counts calls that already happened",
  "Branded searches from people who would have found the practice anyway",
  "Referrals from existing patients or other dentists"

## Common mistakes in measuring dental marketing return

| Mistake | What it hides | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Reporting ROAS based on a fixed lead value | Whether leads became paying patients | Report ROAS on collected revenue as well, and label which is which |
| Counting management fees as overhead, not marketing cost | Part of the true acquisition cost | Include every cost that would end if the channel ended |
| Using production instead of collections | Write-offs and unpaid balances | Use collected revenue within a stated window |
| Treating revenue as return | Clinical and supply costs | Convert to contribution before calculating ROI |
| Changing definitions mid-year | Real trends | Fix definitions and note any change in the report |
| Judging a channel on one month | Normal variation and collection delay | Review trends over several months with the same window |
