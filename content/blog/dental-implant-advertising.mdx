<KeyAnswer>Advertise implants by paying only for searches that signal a treatment decision, sending them to a page that explains the process, the consultation and the money honestly, and judging the campaign on qualified, attended consultations rather than leads. Stay inside Google's misrepresentation rules: no improbable outcomes, no fear-based copy, no prices the page cannot back up.</KeyAnswer>

Implants sit in an awkward place for paid search. The treatment is high-value, so each new case can justify meaningful spend. It is also a considered purchase with a long decision window, a wide range of costs and many people who are researching, not deciding. A campaign that treats every "dental implants" click as a buyer usually fills the schedule with free or low-commitment consultations that rarely turn into treatment. This article covers how to structure implant campaigns around intent, what the landing page must do, how to talk about cost without breaking Google's rules or lending law, and how to measure consultation quality. Bidding, match types and budget mechanics are covered in [the Google Ads management service](/services/dental-google-ads-management/) and the related articles linked at the end.

## Which implant searches signal a treatment decision

Not every implant search is a patient looking for a provider. Some are people comparing materials, some are researching a relative's options, some are bargain-hunting abroad, some are dental students. Before writing a single ad, sort the search terms you could buy into intent tiers, and decide which tiers deserve their own budget.

| Intent tier | Example research candidates | What the searcher probably wants | How to treat it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local provider, decision stage | "implant dentist near Pasadena", "dental implant consultation", "same practice implants and crown" | A practice to evaluate them | Core campaign, highest bids, tight geography |
| Specific treatment type | "full arch implants", "implant-supported denture", "single tooth implant" | Information on one option, often with a provider in mind | Separate ad groups with matching landing sections |
| Cost and financing | "dental implant cost", "implants payment plan" | A number and a way to pay | Bid cautiously; only send to a page that answers the cost question honestly |
| Comparison and research | "implants vs bridge", "how long do implants take" | Education | Usually content and SEO, not paid; test only with low budgets |
| Out-of-scope | "cheap implants Mexico", "free implants", "implant dentist jobs", "implant course" | Something you do not offer | Negative keywords |

The keyword examples above are research candidates to check in your own account's search terms report, not volume claims. The point of the table is the treatment column: the decision-stage tier should never share an ad group or a landing page with the cost tier, because the two groups need different reassurance and convert into consultations of very different quality. The mechanics of excluding the out-of-scope tier without losing good searches are covered in [how to build dental negative keyword lists](/blog/dental-google-ads-negative-keywords/).

### Geography matters more for implants than for routine care

Many people will travel further for implants than for a cleaning, but not infinitely, and not to a practice whose consultation process they do not understand. Start with the radius from which your existing implant patients actually come, if you can pull it from your practice management system as aggregate postal-code counts, and expand only when the qualified-consult rate holds.

## What Google's ad policies mean for implant campaigns

Implant advertising is not one of the categories Google singles out in its healthcare policy, but several general policies apply with particular force to elective treatment.

On 8 November 2026, Google's healthcare and medicines policy focused on prescription drug services, restricted drug terms, unapproved substances, speculative and experimental treatments, clinical trial recruitment, addiction services and health insurance, among others. It says ads and destinations are expected to follow local laws and industry standards, and it notes that ads exclusively for dental insurance coverage are not restricted. Dental procedures such as implants were not listed as a restricted category.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-healthcare-policy"]} />

The misrepresentation policy is the one most relevant to implant ad copy. It prohibits failing to clearly disclose the payment model or full expense, pricing practices that create a misleading impression of cost, clickbait or sensationalist imagery, ads that use negative life events such as illness to pressure immediate action, claims that present an improbable result as the likely outcome, and promotions that are unavailable or hard to find at the destination.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-misrepresentation"]} />

In practice, that translates into a short list of things implant ads should not do:

- Promise a result ("a perfect smile in one day") as the typical outcome.
- Lead with a price that only applies to a narrow case the page does not explain.
- Use shame, fear of ageing or fear of illness as the hook.
- Advertise a "free consultation" or offer that the landing page or front desk does not honour as written.
- Show before-and-after imagery that has been edited or that the practice cannot attribute to its own patients with consent.

Whether a claim is clinically accurate is a separate question from whether it is permitted. Clinical wording on implant pages and ads should be approved by the treating dentist, and state dental board advertising rules may add requirements that Google's policies do not mention.

### Audience features may not be available

Google lists health as a sensitive interest category. Advertisers promoting products and services in sensitive interest categories cannot use advertiser-curated audiences, which include customer match, your data segments (formerly remarketing), lookalike segments and custom segments; predefined Google audiences remain available because sensitive signals are excluded from them.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-personalized-restricted"]} />

Google decides how a campaign is classified, not the advertiser. The safe planning assumption is that an implant campaign must work on search intent and location alone, without retargeting people who visited the implant page.

### Landing pages must work for the crawler and the patient

Google's destination requirements disapprove pages that do not function on common devices, that are unnecessarily difficult to navigate, that redirect to a different domain or that exist mainly to send users elsewhere. Phone numbers in call assets must be in service, connect to the advertised business and have voicemail.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-destination-requirements"]} />

## Building an implant landing page that pre-qualifies

The landing page has two jobs that pull in opposite directions: make it easy to request a consultation, and make it clear enough what implant treatment involves that people who are not ready, not a fit or not able to fund it self-select out. A page that only does the first job produces volume; a page that does both produces consultations the treatment coordinator is glad to see.

Google's Quality Score diagnostic includes landing page experience, described as how relevant and useful the page is to people who click. It is a diagnostic rather than a performance target, but a below-average rating on that component is a prompt to check whether the page answers the search that triggered the ad.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-quality-score"]} />

### The sections a pre-qualifying implant page needs

1. **A headline that matches the ad group.** A full-arch searcher should land on full-arch content, not a generic implant overview.
2. **Who will see them.** The clinician who does the evaluation and, if the practice refers surgical phases, an honest description of who does what. Do not imply in-house services that are referred out.
3. **What the consultation involves.** How long it takes, whether imaging is part of it, whether there is a fee, and what the patient leaves with (a written plan, a cost estimate, financing options).
4. **The process in plain stages.** A non-clinical outline approved by the dentist: evaluation, planning, treatment phases, follow-up. No timelines presented as typical unless the dentist confirms them.
5. **Cost and payment.** How cost is determined, what changes it, which payment options exist and what the consultation estimate will include. More on this below.
6. **Insurance reality.** Implant coverage differs from plan to plan; say that it varies and that the team will check the patient's benefits, rather than implying it is covered.
7. **One primary action.** A consultation request form or call button, repeated after the process and cost sections, with call assets scheduled to the hours someone answers.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-call-assets"]} />

### Qualifying questions on the form

Two or three optional questions can improve consultation quality without hurting completion much: which option the person is interested in (single tooth, several teeth, full arch, not sure), whether they have had a recent evaluation elsewhere, and their preferred way to discuss payment. Keep these to categories and avoid inviting medical history in a marketing form; clinical details belong in the practice's intake process, not in an ad-tracking pipeline.

## How to show cost and financing honestly in implant ads

Cost is the question most implant searchers have and the one practices are most nervous about answering. Ignoring it pushes people to competitors who answer it; answering it badly creates policy and legal exposure.

Federal lending rules add a specific trap for monthly-payment advertising. Under Regulation Z § 1026.24, if a credit advertisement states certain triggering terms (the amount or percentage of a downpayment, the number of payments or repayment period, the amount of any payment, or the amount of any finance charge), it must also state the downpayment, the terms of repayment and the annual percentage rate.

<SourceNote ids={["cfpb-reg-z-1026-24"]} />

An ad that says "implants from $199 a month" may therefore need far more disclosure than fits in a search ad. Whether and how this rule applies to a practice promoting a third-party lender's plan is a question for the practice's counsel and the lender, which usually supplies approved advertising language. A practical approach is to keep payment specifics off the ad itself ("payment options available, discussed at your consultation") and put lender-approved wording on the landing page.

<Callout title="Cost wording that tends to hold up" tone="note">
Explain what drives the cost (number of teeth, whether additional procedures are needed, the type of restoration), say the consultation produces a written estimate, list payment options by name only, and show any figure only with what it includes. Have the dentist and, for financing language, counsel or the lender approve the final copy.
</Callout>

## A consultation-quality scorecard for implant campaigns

The single most useful thing a practice can do before launching implant ads is agree, in writing, on what a good consultation is. Without that, the marketing report counts forms, the front desk counts calls, the treatment coordinator remembers the disappointing ones, and nobody can say whether the campaign works.

## Worked example: two implant campaigns, judged two ways

<Illustrative title="A two-dentist practice comparing implant campaign structures over one quarter">
The practice spent the same amount on two approaches over three months.

**Campaign A** bid broadly on "dental implants" and "implant cost" terms, used an ad promising a low starting monthly payment, and sent everyone to a short page with a "free consultation" form. It produced 120 form fills and calls classified as implant inquiries, 70 scheduled consultations and 38 attended. The dentist judged 15 of those reasonable candidates, plans were presented to 13, and 4 accepted treatment within 90 days.

**Campaign B** bid only on decision-stage and treatment-type terms within a tighter radius, ran ads describing the consultation process without a price, and sent visitors to a page with process, cost-driver and payment-option sections plus one optional qualifying question. It produced 55 implant inquiries, 40 scheduled, 31 attended, 24 candidates, 22 plans presented and 9 accepted.

Judged on cost per lead, Campaign A looks more than twice as efficient. Judged on cost per accepted plan, Campaign B is more than twice as efficient, and it used fewer consultation slots to get there. The numbers are invented; the pattern to look for in your own data is a large gap between inquiries and qualified consultations.
</Illustrative>

The useful lesson is not that broad campaigns are bad. It is that a campaign's quality only becomes visible when the practice records the later stages. Without the scorecard, Campaign A would have been scaled.

## Tradeoffs and common mistakes in implant advertising

**Optimising the ad account to the wrong conversion.** If the account's primary conversion is "any form submission" or "any call over a short duration", automated bidding will find more of whatever is cheapest to produce. Once there is enough data, import qualified or attended consultations as the conversion that bidding learns from; until then, keep implant campaigns on a conversion definition that at least excludes existing-patient calls. [How to measure calls without counting every call as a new patient](/blog/dental-call-tracking/) explains the classification step.

**Sending implant traffic to the homepage.** The homepage is built for everyone. Implant searchers need the process and cost sections immediately.

**"Free consultation" as the only message.** It can raise inquiry volume while lowering attendance and candidacy. If the practice offers one, describe what it includes so the offer pre-qualifies rather than just attracting.

**Scaling before capacity exists.** An implant campaign that books consultations three or four weeks out loses people to practices that can see them sooner. Check the consult schedule before raising budgets; [setting a dental ad budget from qualified inquiries and attended visits](/blog/dental-google-ads-budget/) covers the capacity side of the calculation.

**Copying claims from competitors' ads.** Other ads in the auction are not evidence that a claim is allowed. An ad you see may not have been reviewed yet, may run under a different account history, or may simply be disapproved next week.

**Ignoring the call path.** Implant callers often have more questions than routine callers. If the person answering cannot explain the consultation, the campaign's effort is lost on the phone.
