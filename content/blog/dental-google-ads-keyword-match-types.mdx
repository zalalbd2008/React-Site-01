## How Google defines the three match types today

Google's current definitions all describe meaning rather than exact wording, which is why advice written a few years ago can mislead.

Two points in Google's description change how an account should be built. First, Google says broader match types capture all the queries of narrower ones: a broad keyword matches everything its phrase and exact equivalents would, plus related searches. Running "dental implants" in all three types is therefore mostly duplication. Second, broad match is the default. A keyword typed without quotes or brackets is broad match, which is how many practice-built accounts end up matching far more than intended.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-match-types"]} />

### Close variants apply to every match type

The search terms report labels matches such as "Exact match (close variant)". Google describes close variants as including singular and plural forms, acronyms, stem words, abbreviations, accents and variants with the same meaning. For a dental account, `[emergency dentist]` can match a search worded differently that Google judges to mean the same thing. Exact match no longer means exact words, and the only way to know what it matched is to read the report.

## Why dental searches make match type decisions harder

Dental services have neighbours that look similar to a matching system but mean very different things to a practice:

- **Patient versus profession.** Searches about dental assistant jobs, hygiene school or continuing education share vocabulary with patient searches.
- **Service versus adjacent service.** Implants, dentures, bridges and "teeth in a day" overlap in language but differ in what the practice offers, the consultation it needs and its value.
- **Need versus research.** A search for a cost or a definition is earlier in the decision than "implant dentist" plus a neighbourhood, and may still be worth reaching, but not at the same bid.
- **Available versus unavailable.** An emergency search at 11 p.m. is worthless to a practice that cannot answer the phone until morning.

Broad match decides between these using signals Google controls. Phrase and exact decide more from the keyword you chose. Neither is right by default; the question is which signals the account can trust.

## What broad match needs before it is worth using

Google is direct about this: "It's critical to use Smart Bidding with broad match." The reasoning Google gives is that every search is different and bids should reflect auction-time signals, so that across the wider set of searches broad match reaches, the account only competes in the right auctions at the right bid. Google's broad match guide repeats the point and lists more data for Smart Bidding as a benefit.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-match-types", "google-ads-broad-match-guide"]} />

That makes broad match only as good as the conversion actions Smart Bidding is told to pursue. Google's primary and secondary conversion settings determine which actions are used for bidding: primary actions feed the Conversions column and bidding, and secondary actions are observation only. If the primary conversion is "any call over 10 seconds" or "any form view", Smart Bidding will find more of those, which is not the same as more new patients.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-primary-secondary-conversions"]} />

Volume matters too. Google's Smart Bidding guidance recommends evaluating performance over longer periods with at least 30 conversions, such as a month or longer. A single-office practice running a modest implant campaign may not produce 30 genuine inquiries in a month, which means the bidding system is learning slowly and any evaluation of broad match is noisy.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-smart-bidding"]} />

## What happens when several keywords could match one search

Practices often find a search matched by an unexpected keyword or ad group. Google's prioritisation rules, as published on the retrieval date, run in this order:

1. **An exact match keyword identical to the search wins.** If someone searches the exact text of an eligible exact match keyword, that Search campaign is prioritised over broad or phrase keywords and over Performance Max.
2. **Phrase and broad keywords identical to the search** share priority with an identical Performance Max search theme.
3. **Relevance.** If a search could match keywords in several ad groups, only the most relevant ad groups, as judged by Google's AI, are considered.
4. **Ad Rank** decides among equal candidates.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-match-types"]} />

This is the main remaining reason to keep exact match keywords alongside broad ones. If "implant dentist" plus your city is the search you most want routed to the implant ad group and its dedicated landing page, an identical exact keyword in that ad group is the most dependable way to get it there. Google also lists reasons a keyword may show in Performance Max instead of Search, including the Search campaign being limited by budget.

### AI Max widens matching further

Google's AI Max for Search campaigns adds "search term matching", which Google describes as expanding existing keywords using broad match and keywordless technology, learning from keywords, creatives and URLs. It is turned on with AI Max and can be switched off at ad group level. AI Max also brings brand controls, URL controls and locations of interest. Google notes AI Max will not be effective when a campaign is limited by budget. For a practice, turning on AI Max is effectively a decision to use broad-style matching, so the same prerequisites apply.

## Reading the search terms report as the control loop

Whatever the match types, the search terms report is where you find out what the account actually bought. Google's report shows the search term, the keyword that triggered the ad (the Keyword column, which is not shown by default and must be added) and the match type of the search term relative to the keyword. Two quirks matter:

- The search term match type can differ from the keyword's match type. A broad keyword that matched an identical search is reported as exact match.
- Terms without enough query activity are omitted for privacy. Search terms insights groups some of them into themes, but the report is never a complete list of searches you paid for.

The example shows the practical difference between match types: broad match created the denture and course matches, and the review turned them into decisions. Without a weekly review, broad match runs unattended, which is where most of its waste comes from.

## Tradeoffs and common mistakes

**Exact and phrase give control at the cost of reach.** They miss legitimate searches phrased in ways you did not predict, and they need more keyword maintenance. **Broad match gives reach at the cost of predictability**, and it shifts control from the keyword list to the conversion data. Neither choice is about the keyword alone.

Common mistakes:

- Leaving keywords at the default broad match without realising it, while bidding on clicks rather than conversions.
- Switching to broad match when the primary conversion is a page view, a short call or a directions click.
- Treating exact match as word-for-word and skipping search term reviews on exact-only campaigns.
- Duplicating every keyword in three match types, which adds complexity Google says is unnecessary.
- Excluding adjacent intents such as dentures or cost questions that the practice could serve with the right ad group and page.
- Judging a match type change after a few days. Allow enough conversions to evaluate, and change one thing at a time.

Negatives are the other half of this system, and they behave differently from positive match types. Google states that negative keywords do not match close variants, so a negative for one form of a word will not block its synonyms or plural. That asymmetry is the subject of the [negative keywords article](/blog/dental-google-ads-negative-keywords/).
