<KeyAnswer>Offer real-time online booking for simple, predictable appointment types that you can protect in reserved schedule blocks, ideally with a tool that writes directly into your practice management system. Use a request form where the front desk must screen, triage or verify first: emergencies, complex consultations, insurance-sensitive bookings and tightly managed schedules. Offering both, each clearly labelled, is a reasonable default.</KeyAnswer>

The decision is less about patient preference than about schedule control. Real-time booking hands part of the schedule to the public under rules the practice sets in advance. A request form keeps every decision with the front desk but adds a delay and a callback that someone has to make. This article covers how to choose between them and how to make whichever you choose work on a phone. If you are diagnosing why your current website produces few appointment requests at all, start with [the mobile conversion audit](/blog/dental-website-conversion-audit/).

## What each option actually commits the practice to

**Real-time online booking** shows available times and lets the patient take one. The practice commits to keeping the online availability accurate, defining which appointment types the public may book, and dealing with bookings that turn out to be the wrong type, length or provider.

**An appointment request form** collects the patient's details and preferences and sends them to the practice. The practice commits to responding fast enough that the patient does not book elsewhere first, during the hours the patient is likely to be waiting.

**A hybrid** offers real-time booking for a narrow set of appointment types and a request form, or a phone call, for everything else.

| Factor | Real-time booking | Request form |
|---|---|---|
| Patient effort | One session; appointment confirmed immediately | Submit, then wait for a call, text or email |
| Schedule control | Set in advance by rules and blocks | Decided case by case by the front desk |
| Front-desk workload | Lower per booking; higher for fixing wrong bookings | A callback for every request |
| PMS integration | Matters a great deal; without it, double entry and clashes | Optional; staff enter appointments as they schedule |
| Insurance verification | After the booking, sometimes forcing a reschedule | Before the appointment is confirmed |
| Emergencies and triage | Poor fit without screening questions | Good fit if responses are fast |
| Failure mode | Wrong appointment type, provider or length | Slow or missed callbacks |
| After-hours demand | Captured and confirmed | Captured but unconfirmed until morning |

Neither column wins outright. The right answer depends on which failure mode your practice handles more easily.

## Schedule control and practice management integration

The most important technical question about any booking tool is what happens to the appointment after the patient clicks confirm. There are broadly three patterns, and vendors do not always make clear which one they provide.

1. **Direct write-back.** The tool reads real availability from the practice management system and writes the appointment into it. Double-booking is unlikely and nobody re-keys the details.
2. **Synchronised copy.** The tool keeps its own calendar and synchronises with the practice management system at intervals. Gaps between syncs can allow clashes.
3. **Notification only.** The tool shows a calendar but simply emails or messages the front desk with the booking, which staff then enter. From the patient's side it looks like a confirmed booking; operationally it is a request form.

Pattern 3 is not wrong, but the confirmation message must match reality. Telling a patient "your appointment is confirmed" when a person still has to accept it creates no-shows and complaints when the slot turns out to be taken.

Online availability should be built from blocks the practice is willing to give away. A common structure is to reserve specific new-patient exam slots per provider per week, offer them online, and release any unbooked ones to the front desk a day or two ahead. That keeps the hygienist's and the dentist's production days intact while still capturing after-hours demand.

## New patients and existing patients need different flows

Most of the friction in dental booking comes from treating new and existing patients the same way. They want different things and the practice needs different information from each.

## Insurance verification without breaking the booking

Insurance is where real-time booking and front-desk reality collide most often. A patient books, the practice later finds the plan is out of network or the benefits are exhausted, and the appointment must be rescheduled or the conversation about cost happens at the front desk.

Three habits reduce that friction:

- **Collect the carrier and subscriber details at booking**, but do not require group numbers or member IDs that patients often do not have to hand; ask for those in the follow-up.
- **Say clearly that coverage will be confirmed** before the visit, and that the team will contact the patient if anything changes.
- **Restrict online booking for plan types the practice handles differently**, or route those patients to a request form, if your participation is complex.

A request form handles insurance more gracefully because the front desk can verify before confirming. That is one of the strongest arguments for keeping a form alongside real-time booking.

## No-shows, cancellations and the cost of easy booking

A frequent concern is that booking that is easy to make is also easy to ignore. We are not aware of a published, dental-specific comparison of no-show rates between online bookings and phone bookings with a clear sample and method, so treat any figure you hear as a hypothesis to test in your own schedule.

What a practice can control is the confirmation and reminder path:

- Send an immediate confirmation that states the date, time, location, provider and what to bring.
- Send reminders by the channels the patient chose, with an easy way to cancel or reschedule rather than simply not arriving.
- For high-value slots such as long consultations, call to confirm, which also gives the team a chance to answer questions.
- Track no-shows by booking source in the practice management system, so the decision about online booking rests on your data.

## Making booking and request forms work on a phone

Whichever option you choose, most people will use it on a phone. The same accessibility and usability principles apply to a request form and a booking widget, but widgets are harder to fix because they are usually third-party code inside a frame.

WCAG 2.2 asks for labels or instructions where input is required, for fields that collect user information to expose their purpose programmatically (for example through autocomplete), and for pointer targets of at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels with exceptions. web.dev's forms guidance explains how correct autocomplete attributes let browsers fill names, emails and phone numbers so users do not retype them.

<SourceNote ids={["w3c-wcag22-labels-or-instructions", "w3c-wcag22-identify-input-purpose", "w3c-wcag22-target-size-minimum", "webdev-learn-forms-autofill"]} />

Booking widgets also tend to be heavy. Interaction to Next Paint measures how quickly a page responds to taps across the visit; a calendar that freezes for a moment after each tap feels broken on a mid-range phone even when it eventually works.

<SourceNote ids={["webdev-inp"]} />

Before committing to a vendor, test its widget yourself on a phone over mobile data, with the screen reader turned on for at least one pass, and with keyboard navigation on a laptop.

## Booking links on Google Business Profile

Google Business Profiles can carry booking links. Google's help page says profiles can include up to 10 links per category with one set as preferred, that third-party booking links can appear automatically through partners, and that such third-party links must be removed by contacting the provider, which is required to process opt-out requests within 5 business days.

Check what booking link your profile actually shows. An old vendor's link, or a third-party directory's booking button, can send patients into a flow the practice no longer uses.

## Worked example: a hybrid set-up for a four-chair practice

<Illustrative title="A general practice adds limited online booking alongside its request form">
The practice has two dentists and two hygienists. It reserves six new-patient exam slots a week online, three per dentist, all on days with spare hygiene capacity. Unbooked online slots return to the front desk 48 hours ahead. Emergencies, implant and orthodontic consultations, and anything the patient labels "not sure" go to a request form that promises a call back the same business day.

Over a quarter the website produces 90 online bookings and 110 form requests. The front desk records, in the practice management system, how each was created and whether the patient attended. Online bookings show a slightly higher no-show rate, but they arrive mostly in the evening, when the phone is closed, and the front desk spends far less time per booking. Form requests take more staff time but include most of the high-value consultations.

The practice keeps both, adds a confirmation call for consultations, and moves one online exam slot to a Saturday. All figures are invented; the method is the point.
</Illustrative>

## Measuring which option works for your practice

Treat completed online bookings and submitted request forms as two separate key events in analytics. Google Analytics calls important actions key events, and Google Ads conversions can be created from them, so keep the definitions distinct rather than merging both into a single "lead".

<SourceNote ids={["ga4-conversions-vs-key-events"]} />

Then, monthly, match each to what happened in the practice management system: scheduled, attended, no-show, cancelled. The comparison that matters is attended new-patient visits per hundred visitors who reached the booking or request page, by source. Phone calls should be measured alongside, using the classification approach in [dental call tracking](/blog/dental-call-tracking/), because many patients who see a booking button still call. Setting up that measurement end to end is the job of [dental conversion tracking](/services/dental-conversion-tracking/).

## Common mistakes

- **Offering every appointment type online.** Wrong-type bookings consume more staff time than they save.
- **Calling a notification tool "online booking".** If staff must accept it, say "request" in the confirmation.
- **Hiding the phone number on the booking page.** Some patients will want to ask a question first.
- **Letting the form inbox go unowned.** A request form is only as good as the callback.
- **Putting tracking tags on booking confirmation pages without review.** Ask counsel how HIPAA applies to booking vendors and to any analytics or advertising tags on pages where patients enter information.
