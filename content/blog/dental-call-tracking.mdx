<KeyAnswer>Track calls by source with tracking numbers, but count them in stages: every call, then calls classified as new-patient opportunities, then calls that booked, then first visits attended. Keep the Business Profile number one that rings the practice directly, keep the main number consistent everywhere, and check recording-consent rules for every state your callers are in before recording.</KeyAnswer>

Call tracking is one of the most useful measurement tools a dental practice can have, because phone calls are a contact path that web analytics alone cannot see. It is also one of the most misread. A report that counts every ringing phone as a lead makes every channel look productive and makes it impossible to decide where to spend. This article covers how to classify calls, how tracking numbers interact with your Google Business Profile and Google Ads, how to think about duration and AI scoring, and where recording-consent law requires caution. Definitions of each funnel stage are in [the dental marketing metrics reference](/resources/dental-marketing-metrics/); building the full measurement system is the job of [dental conversion tracking](/services/dental-conversion-tracking/).

## Why a raw call count overstates new patients

A tracking number records that a call happened, when, for how long, and which source the caller came from. It cannot know who the caller is or why they called. A month of calls to a typical practice line includes:

- New patients wanting an appointment.
- New patients asking a question and not booking yet.
- Existing patients confirming, rescheduling or cancelling.
- Existing patients with billing or insurance questions.
- Insurance companies, labs, suppliers and job applicants.
- Spam, robocalls, wrong numbers and hang-ups.
- The same person calling twice or three times about one issue.

Existing patients also look up the number on the website and Business Profile, so organic search and maps calls mix existing patients with new ones. If those are counted as leads, organic search looks like the most productive channel while possibly producing fewer new patients than the report implies.

## A call classification scheme a front desk can use

Classification is the step that turns call data into marketing data. It can be done by listening to recordings, by front-desk notes, by an automated tool, or by a mix. The categories must be few enough that staff apply them consistently.

The "new patient, not booked" category deserves particular attention. Its reasons tell the practice whether the problem is marketing (wrong people calling), operations (no availability, unanswered questions) or price and insurance fit. That is often the most actionable column in the whole report.

The last stage requires matching calls to appointments. That can be done with a monthly export of new-patient first visits (dates and phone numbers, handled under the practice's privacy procedures) compared with booked calls, or by having the front desk record the referral source at scheduling. Neither is perfect; both are far better than stopping at "calls". The difference between leads, bookings, visits, revenue and profit is unpacked further in [how to tell dental marketing ROI stages apart](/blog/dental-marketing-roi/).

## Duration thresholds and AI call scoring

Google Ads can count a call from an ad as a conversion in two ways: by counting calls that last longer than a minimum duration the advertiser sets, or by having Google's AI analyse call recordings to identify higher-quality leads. Recording-based analysis is limited to calls where both parties are in the U.S. or Canada and can be turned off in account settings; if recording is off or unavailable, the system falls back to duration. Advertisers who track outcomes elsewhere can import calls as a separate conversion action.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ads-call-conversions"]} />

Duration is a crude filter. It removes most hang-ups and wrong numbers, but an existing patient disputing a bill can talk for ten minutes, and a new patient who already knows what they want can book in two. Automated scoring may do better, but it is still a model's judgement about intent, not a record of what happened. For bidding, the most reliable signal is an imported outcome: a call later confirmed as a new-patient booking or attended visit.

In analytics, calls from website visits can be recorded as events and marked as key events; Google Ads conversions can be created from those key events. Keep call key events separate from form and booking events so each path can be judged on its own.

## Dynamic number insertion, NAP consistency and your Business Profile

Dynamic number insertion (DNI) swaps the phone number shown on the website according to how the visitor arrived, so a visitor from a paid ad sees one number and a visitor from organic search sees another. It is how call tracking attributes website calls to sources. It also creates a risk: the practice's name, address and phone number (NAP) appearing inconsistently across the web.

### What Google's Business Profile rules say about phone numbers

Google's guidelines for representing a business ask for a phone number that connects to the individual business location, a local number rather than a central call centre where possible, a number under the direct control of the business, and no numbers that redirect or refer users to anyone other than the actual business.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

A Business Profile can list a primary number and up to two additional numbers.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-phone-numbers"]} />

Read together, those rules allow a tracking number on the profile if it rings straight through to that location, is controlled by the practice, and is not a referral service. One approach, which comes from call tracking practice rather than from Google's documentation, is to put a dedicated tracking number in the primary field and the office line in an additional field, so the office number still matches the website and directories. Whether to do that is a judgement call; the alternative is to keep the office number as primary and measure profile calls through the profile's own reporting and a tagged website link.

### Rules that keep DNI from damaging consistency

- **Keep a single canonical number for each location** and use it in structured data, the footer's static fallback, directories and printed material.
- **Swap numbers only in the visible page content**, not in schema markup or citations.
- **Use a different tracking number per channel**, not per page, unless you have the call volume to make page-level numbers meaningful.
- **Own the numbers.** Tracking numbers should be in an account the practice controls, so they can be ported if the provider changes. A number that disappears when a contract ends breaks every place it was published.
- **Retire numbers carefully.** Old tracking numbers linger in directories and old ads; keep them forwarding for a long period after retirement.

### DNI and Google Ads phone verification

Google Ads verifies phone numbers used in call assets. Its help page says that where a website uses DNI or third-party call tracking, Google cannot verify the number by scanning the site's source code, and the advertiser must prove domain ownership instead, either by linking Search Console to Google Ads or by placing the Google tag directly in the site's raw HTML rather than through a tag manager. Call assets can also be scheduled to show only when the practice is answering phones.

## Call recording consent: a cautious summary

Recording calls is valuable for classification and training. It is also regulated, and the rules depend on where the parties are, not only where the practice is.

Under federal law, 18 U.S.C. § 2511(2)(d) provides that a person not acting under color of law may intercept a communication where that person is a party or one party has given prior consent, unless the interception is for a criminal or tortious purpose. This is often described as one-party consent.

<SourceNote ids={["usc-18-2511"]} />

Several states are stricter. Florida's statute makes interception lawful when all parties to the communication have given prior consent. In California, a summary by the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press describes Penal Code § 632 as requiring all parties' consent to record a confidential communication, including by telephone, and § 632.7 as requiring all-party consent for calls involving a cellular or cordless phone regardless of confidentiality.

<SourceNote ids={["fl-statutes-934-03", "rcfp-recording-guide-california"]} />

By the same organisation's summaries, Texas and Georgia generally permit recording by a party to the call or with one party's consent.

<SourceNote ids={["rcfp-recording-guide-texas", "rcfp-recording-guide-georgia"]} />

<Callout title="What this means in practice" tone="caution">
These are summaries, not legal advice, and the details (what counts as confidential, how cross-state calls are treated, what notice is sufficient) vary. A practice whose callers may be in an all-party-consent state, including any practice that advertises across state lines, should ask its counsel before enabling recording. A clear announcement at the start of every recorded call is a common safeguard counsel may recommend. Recordings can also contain patient health information, so access, retention and the vendor relationship belong in the practice's HIPAA review.
</Callout>

## Worked example: one month of calls, read two ways

<Illustrative title="A single-location practice reviews 400 tracked calls">
The call tracking report shows 400 calls: 160 from organic search and the Business Profile, 140 from Google Ads, 60 from a directory listing and 40 from a mailer.

After classification and deduplication, the picture changes. Of the 160 organic and profile calls, 95 are existing patients, 15 are vendors or spam and 50 are new-patient opportunities, 30 of whom booked. Of the 140 ad calls, 25 are existing patients who searched the practice name and clicked the ad, 20 are spam or wrong numbers, and 95 are new-patient opportunities, 48 of whom booked. The directory produced 12 opportunities and 5 bookings; the mailer 14 opportunities and 9 bookings.

Matching to the practice management system a month later, 72 of the 92 booked new patients attended. Of the 81 opportunities that did not book, 33 cited no appointment within two weeks.

Read raw, organic search was the largest channel. Read classified, paid search produced the most new-patient bookings, the mailer had the highest booking rate, and the biggest single problem was availability, not marketing. All numbers are invented.
</Illustrative>

The example also shows why branded searches matter: existing patients who click a paid ad on the practice's own name can make a paid campaign look busier than it is. Classification exposes that.

## Common mistakes in dental call tracking

- **Reporting calls as leads.** Always report the classified stages alongside the raw count.
- **Putting tracking numbers in citations and schema.** It fragments NAP consistency and outlives the campaign.
- **Letting the vendor own the numbers.** Ports are hard when the account is not yours.
- **Ignoring missed calls.** A missed new-patient call with no callback is a lost patient that never appears in the booking data.
- **Turning on recording without checking consent rules.** The default setting in a tool is not legal advice.
- **Optimising ad bidding to duration alone** once better outcome data exists.
