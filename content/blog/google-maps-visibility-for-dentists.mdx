<KeyAnswer>A dental practice usually fails to appear in Google Maps because the profile is unverified, suspended or duplicated; because its category, name or details do not match what patients search for; because the searcher is too far away; or because nearby practices are more prominent. Check them in that order. Google's local ranking help describes three main factors, relevance, distance and prominence, and says no one can pay for a better local ranking.</KeyAnswer>

"We don't show up in Maps" covers several different situations. The profile may be missing entirely, it may appear only when someone searches the practice's name, or it may appear for "dentist" near the building but disappear a mile away. This article sets out how to tell them apart and what Google's own rules say about each. It is written for a single-office practice; groups with several offices face extra questions about consistency across locations, covered on our [multi-location local SEO page](/services/local-seo-multi-location-clinics/).

## First, check that the profile can appear at all

Before thinking about ranking, confirm that Google is showing the profile. Four conditions stop a profile appearing or weaken it badly.

**It is not verified.** Business Profile Help says a business must be verified before its owner can edit details such as the name or hours and interact with customers; verification is how the practice gains ownership of the profile. Verification methods are chosen automatically by Google and can include phone or text, email, video and postcard. Performance data is available only for verified profiles, so an unverified practice cannot see how its listing performs.

**It is suspended or disabled.** A suspended profile is not visible to the public. The fix is an appeal, not a new profile; Google explicitly says not to create a new Business Profile for the same business. Our guide to [what to prepare before appealing a suspension](/blog/dental-google-business-profile-suspension/) covers the evidence.

**It has been treated as a duplicate.** Google's duplicates page says that if a profile is considered a duplicate, it won't show on Search or Maps. Duplicates arise when a practice moves and someone creates a new profile instead of updating the address, when an old owner's profile survives an acquisition, or when separate profiles are created for different services.

**Practitioner profiles are competing with the practice.** Google's guidelines name dentists as individual practitioners, who may have their own profiles in some circumstances. When a practice profile and several dentist profiles share an address, they can compete for the same searches. The rules for when each is appropriate are covered in [practice versus practitioner profiles](/blog/dentist-practice-google-business-profiles/).

## How Google decides which dentists appear in the map results

Google's Business Profile Help names three main factors and describes each briefly. Knowing what each one covers tells you which problems a practice can fix and which it cannot.

### Relevance: does the profile match the search?

Google describes relevance as how well a profile matches what someone searches for, and advises complete and detailed business information. For a dental practice, the main relevance levers are:

- **The primary category.** Business Profile Help says the categories you select affect your local ranking. Its guidelines ask for the most specific category that represents the main business, in the form "this business IS a", not "this business HAS a". A general practice is a dentist; a practice limited to orthodontics is an orthodontist. Choosing a specialist category the practice does not genuinely represent invites edits and reports.
- **Additional categories, used sparingly.** The guidelines say to use as few categories as possible and not to use them solely as keywords. List individual treatments in the services section instead.
- **The business name.** It must reflect the real-world name used on signage, website and stationery. Google's examples of what a name must not include cover marketing taglines and service or product information.
- **Website content.** Business Profile Help says the information shown about a business is compiled from several sources, including crawled web content such as the business's official website. A website that clearly describes the practice's services supports the profile.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-local-ranking", "google-gbp-categories", "google-gbp-guidelines", "google-gbp-sourcing"]} />

### Distance: how far is the searcher?

Distance is how far the business is from the person searching; if the searcher does not share a location, Google uses what it knows about where they are. A practice cannot change its distance from anyone. What it can do is make sure the address and map pin are accurate. Business Profile Help explains how to enter a complete street address, include the suite number and adjust the pin if Google cannot find the building.

This is why visibility in Maps changes from street to street, and why a search from the front desk is the least representative test there is.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-local-ranking", "google-gbp-address"]} />

### Prominence: how well known is the practice?

Google describes prominence as how well-known a business is, based partly on information such as how many websites link to it and how many reviews it has, and says more reviews and positive ratings can help local ranking. For practices, prominence is where established competitors usually have the advantage: years of reviews, mentions by local organisations and references across the web.

Reviews must be genuine. Google's prohibited content policy forbids incentives for reviews and selectively soliciting positive reviews, and its restrictions page says that after fake-engagement violations Google may stop a profile receiving new reviews or unpublish existing ones for a period. A review programme that breaks these rules can reduce prominence rather than build it. The process for asking every patient fairly is covered in [requesting reviews without incentives or gating](/blog/dental-review-request-process/).

## How to test Maps visibility properly

Because distance shapes results, a single search tells you very little. A structured test gives a far more useful picture. The method below needs no paid tools, although rank-tracking tools that simulate searches from a grid of points can do the same at scale.

<Checklist title="A simple proximity test for a dental practice" items={[
  "Choose three or four searches patients actually use, such as dentist, emergency dentist and family dentist",
  "Pick five to eight locations across the area you want to serve: near the office, at the edges, and in neighbourhoods where patients live",
  "Search from each location on a phone with location on, signed out or in a private window",
  "Record whether the practice appears in the map results and which practices appear instead",
  "Repeat at the same points monthly, on a similar day and time",
  "Compare with Business Profile performance data, which shows views, searches, calls and direction requests"
]} />

The goal is not a single "rank". It is a map of where the practice is visible, where it is not, and which competitors take its place in each area. That tells you whether the gap is about relevance (absent everywhere), distance (absent only far away, which is expected) or prominence (absent where a specific competitor dominates).

Remember what the profile's own data measures. Business Profile Help says the calls metric counts taps on the call button, the searches metric is updated at the start of each month, and performance data includes views and actions from both organic results and Google Ads.

## Dealing with duplicates and spam

**Your own duplicates.** Google says a profile considered a duplicate will not show on Search or Maps, and that a practice that moves should update the existing profile's address rather than create a new one. Before removing or merging anything, check which profile holds the reviews: removal deletes a profile's content, and merging can lose review replies. The removal, merge and ownership routes are compared step by step in [practice versus practitioner profiles](/blog/dentist-practice-google-business-profiles/).

**Other businesses at your address.** Google allows distinct businesses at one address to have their own profiles if they have different names and clearly visible differences in signage. If Google wrongly merged two distinct businesses, you may be asked for evidence of permanent signage showing both.

**Competitors breaking the rules.** Google says that if a profile is inaccurate or should be removed, anyone can suggest an edit or flag it. For a competitor whose name includes treatments or a city that do not appear on its signage, compare the name with its building and website before suggesting an edit, and stick to facts. Google decides whether to act on a suggestion, so treat reporting as worthwhile for clear violations, not as a primary strategy.

## A worked example: visible next door, invisible across town

<Illustrative title="A general practice that only appears within a mile of the office">
A two-dentist general practice says it "doesn't show in Maps". The owner searches from the office and sees the practice third in the map results, so the complaint seems wrong.

The proximity test from six points tells a different story. The practice appears near the office and in one adjoining neighbourhood. In three neighbourhoods where many existing patients live, it does not appear at all; two other practices appear in every one of them.

Checking the profile reveals two issues. The primary category is a specialist category the practice adopted after adding aligner treatment, although most of its work is general dentistry. And each dentist has a practitioner profile at the same address, one of which uses the practice name.

The plan: change the primary category to the accurate general category and list aligners as a service; rename the practitioner profile to the dentist's name only, following Google's practitioner rules; and start a review request process for all patients after visits. The two dominant competitors have several times as many reviews, so the practice expects distance-related gaps to narrow slowly, not close.

## Tradeoffs and common mistakes

- **Chasing distance.** No profile edit changes how far the practice is from a searcher. Expect weaker visibility at the edges of the area, and use advertising if those areas matter commercially.
- **Category churn.** Business Profile Help warns that changing a category may require re-verification. Decide on the category carefully and leave it.
- **Keyword names.** Adding treatments or a city to the name may seem to work briefly but breaks Google's naming rules and invites edits and reports.
- **Creating a new profile to escape problems.** Google says not to create a new profile for the same business, whether after a suspension or a move.
- **Buying or filtering reviews.** Incentives and selective solicitation are prohibited and can lead to restrictions.
- **Ignoring the website.** The profile links to the website and Google uses web content to understand the business. A thin website weakens relevance.

## Sources and limitations

This diagnosis is based on Google Business Profile Help pages checked on 8 November 2026. Google does not publish how much each factor weighs and keeps its local algorithm confidential, so the order of checks here is a practical model, not Google's ranking formula. Google's local ranking page uses the headings relevance, distance and prominence, although its summary sentence refers to popularity; we follow the headings. Results also vary with the device, the exact wording of the search and whether the searcher has shared a location. Third-party rank-tracking tools simulate searches and can differ from what real patients see.
