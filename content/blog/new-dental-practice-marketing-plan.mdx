<KeyAnswer>Before opening, secure the accounts the practice will own, publish an accurate website, and set up a Google Business Profile with a future opening date once permanent signage is in place. In the first 90 days, run narrow paid search while reviews and map visibility build, request reviews from every real patient, and raise marketing volume only as fast as the phones and schedule can absorb it.</KeyAnswer>

A plan copied from a ten-year-old practice will spend money on the wrong things in the wrong order, because a new practice also has a front desk that has never worked together and a schedule that is empty some weeks and overbooked in others. For the wider business picture of launching a practice, see [marketing for dental startups](/who-we-help/dental-startups/).

## What makes the first 90 days different

Three constraints shape almost every decision in this period:

1. **Platform rules about when a business exists.** Google treats a practice that is not yet seeing patients differently from one that is. Some steps are possible months ahead; others only work once patients can walk in.
2. **No social proof.** Reviews, a track record in the map results and word of mouth all take time. Paid channels and the website have to do work that reviews would normally share.
3. **Untested capacity.** Nobody yet knows how many calls the front desk can answer at lunch, how long new-patient visits really take, or which insurance questions slow bookings down.

The plan below is built around those constraints rather than around a list of channels.

## What Google allows before a dental practice opens

Google's eligibility rule is that a business must make in-person contact with customers during its stated hours to qualify for a Business Profile. A practice that has not opened does not yet do that, which is why Google provides a specific route for it: a business that has not opened can create a profile and set a future opening date of up to one year ahead, with only the year and month required.

Three details matter for planning:

- **Visibility starts 90 days out.** After you enter the date and verify, the profile shows on Google 90 days before the opening date. Before opening you can post updates, add photos and write a description.
- **No reviews until you open.** Users cannot post reviews or photos, or suggest edits, until the business is officially open. Any plan that counts on launch-week reviews before the doors open is not workable.
- **A "Recently opened" label** appears on the profile for 90 days after opening.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-eligibility", "google-gbp-opening-date"]} />

### Verification needs a real, signed location

Verification is the step most tied to the physical build. Google decides which verification methods a profile is offered, and one of them is a live video. For a storefront business, that video must show the surrounding location, permanent signage with the business name matching the profile, and proof that you manage the business, such as access to areas only staff can reach. It must be a live, unedited recording of at least 30 seconds and must not show other people's faces or private information.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-video-verification"]} />

The practical consequence: schedule verification after permanent exterior or suite signage is installed and the office is recognizably a dental practice, not a construction site with a temporary banner. Build that date into the construction timeline. The address should also be the real office location; Google's guidelines do not allow a rented mailing address that the business does not operate from, and they expect permanent signage at any address shown on Google.

<SourceNote ids={["google-gbp-guidelines"]} />

## Decisions to make before opening that are hard to change later

Some choices look administrative but shape the next decade of marketing. Make them deliberately:

- **The practice name as it will appear everywhere.** Google asks businesses to represent themselves as they appear in the real world across signage and stationery. Pick the final name before signage is ordered, and use it identically on the profile, website and listings. Adding service keywords to the profile name is a policy risk, not a tactic.
- **One authoritative phone number.** Decide which number goes on the sign, the profile and the website, and whether calls route to a phone system that can report missed calls.
- **A domain the practice registers and controls.** The registrant on file should be the practice or its owner, with renewal on a card the practice controls.
- **Account ownership.** Create the Business Profile, Google Ads account, Analytics property and Search Console property under practice-owned logins. Agencies and freelancers should be added as users. For the full list of questions to ask a provider, see [how to choose a dental marketing agency](/blog/choosing-a-dental-marketing-agency/).
- **Services you will and will not offer at launch.** If the second dentist who places implants joins in month four, implant pages and ads wait until then.

## Launching the website before the practice opens

Launch as soon as you can publish information that is accurate, not when the site is "finished". Google's SEO starter guide notes that some changes are reflected in hours and others take several months, and suggests waiting a few weeks before judging whether a change had an effect. A new domain launched in the final week before opening gives search engines very little time.

<SourceNote ids={["google-seo-starter-guide"]} />

A pre-opening site does not need dozens of pages. It needs to answer the questions a prospective patient and a search engine will ask:

<Checklist title="What the website needs at launch" items={[
  "Practice name, full address and a map, matching the sign and profile exactly",
  "Opening month or date, updated the day you open",
  "The dentist or dentists: training, languages spoken, and a real photo",
  "Services offered at opening, each with a short page that says what to expect",
  "Insurance plans accepted or the policy on out-of-network patients",
  "Hours, parking and accessibility details",
  "A working call link and a request or booking form that has been tested end to end",
  "A privacy notice and a form that does not ask for clinical details",

Add depth after opening, guided by real questions from the phones. The [website audit checklist](/resources/dental-website-audit-checklist/) is useful once the site has a few weeks of traffic.

## Getting local listings consistent from the start

A new practice has an advantage an established one does not: no old addresses, former names or duplicate profiles to clean up. Protect that. Use one exact version of the name, address and phone number on the Business Profile, the website and the main directories. Choose the fewest categories that describe the core business, as Google's guidelines recommend, rather than every service you might offer. If the practice is a second location of an existing brand, each office needs its own profile; one profile per location is the rule. ## Running ads before reviews exist

Paid search is often the only channel that can produce calls in the first weeks, because it does not depend on a review count or a history in the map results. It also carries more risk for a new practice, because the usual trust signals are missing.

### What changes when there are no reviews

When someone compares an ad for a practice with no reviews against established offices nearby, the landing page has to answer the trust question directly. That means a named dentist with a photo and background, real photos of the office, insurance information, opening hours and a clear next step. A generic stock-photo page will pay for clicks that do not become calls.

### How to keep early campaigns narrow

- **Start with a small set of services** you can see promptly, typically new-patient exams and the core general services you are staffed for.
- **Keep the geography tight.** Google Ads offers a "Presence" location option and suggests considering it when you only want to reach people in specific locations, not people elsewhere who are merely interested in them. For a neighborhood practice, that is usually the relevant question.
- **Run ads only when someone can answer.** Schedule ads around staffed phone hours or route after-hours calls somewhere useful.
- **Count the right things.** A click and a call are not a new patient. Judge campaigns on booked and attended visits as soon as there is enough data.

## Building reviews the right way from the first patient

The first months are when a review habit is formed. Google's policy for Maps content prohibits offering incentives such as discounts or free services for reviews, and prohibits discouraging negative reviews or selectively asking only happy patients. The workable approach is a consistent request to every patient, made the same way each time, with no reward attached.

<SourceNote ids={["google-maps-ugc-prohibited-content"]} />

Remember that reviews only become possible once the profile shows the practice as open. Step-by-step request methods are in [how to request reviews without incentives or gating](/blog/dental-review-request-process/).

## Pacing marketing to the practice's real capacity

A costly mistake in a new practice is not under-marketing but producing calls the team cannot answer and appointments the schedule cannot hold. A missed call from a new patient is a lost first impression, and an urgent inquiry offered a slot three weeks out may go elsewhere.

<Illustrative title="A one-dentist startup planning its first quarter">
A new practice opens with one dentist working four clinical days a week and one hygienist. The owner estimates that, after existing commitments, the schedule can hold about 8 new-patient exams a week in month one, rising to about 15 a week by month three as the hygienist's schedule opens up.

If roughly half of new-patient inquiries become booked appointments and around 80% of those attend, then 8 attended exams a week needs about 20 inquiries a week (20 × 0.5 × 0.8 = 8). Month three's 15 a week needs about 38 inquiries a week.

So the plan is not "maximize leads". It is: aim for about 20 inquiries a week in month one, measure whether the booking and attendance rates hold, and raise paid spend only when the team is answering calls reliably and appointments are available within a few days. If the booking rate turns out to be 30% rather than 50%, the first fix is the phone process, not the ad budget.

All figures here are invented for illustration. Your rates will differ, and in the first weeks they will be noisy.
</Illustrative>

## Measuring from day one

A new practice has a rare chance to set definitions before bad habits form. Decide, before the first ad runs, what counts as an inquiry, a qualified inquiry, a booked appointment and an attended visit, and who records each one. The [dental marketing metrics glossary](/resources/dental-marketing-metrics/) has working definitions. The minimum is:

- calls answered and missed, by hour of day;
- form and booking submissions, and how quickly each was followed up;
- new-patient appointments booked, by source the patient reports or tracking shows;
- attended first visits, from the practice management system.

Expect the first month's numbers to be small and noisy. Use them to find process problems, not to declare a channel a success or failure.

## Tradeoffs and common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts a new practice | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Waiting to launch the website until opening week | Leaves almost no time for crawling and indexing | Publish a short, accurate site as early as details are final |
| Verifying the profile before signage is installed | Verification evidence must show permanent signage and a real office | Tie verification to the signage date in the construction plan |
| Letting a vendor register the domain or create the ad account | The practice may not control its own history later | Create accounts under practice logins and add vendors as users |
| Advertising every service from day one | Spreads a small budget and attracts inquiries the team cannot serve | Start with a few services you can see promptly |
| Asking only happy patients for reviews | Breaches Google's policy on selective solicitation | Ask every patient the same way |
| Judging channels on clicks or calls in month one | Early volumes are small and do not show bookings or attendance | Track booked and attended visits and review trends after several weeks |
