<KeyAnswer>For a dental practice, GEO, AEO and "AI search" all refer to the same practical goal: being found and accurately described when patients use AI features such as Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode or ChatGPT search. Google says its normal SEO requirements apply with no special optimisation. The practice's job is to keep accurate facts crawlable, consistent and easy to cite.</KeyAnswer>

This article explains what the terms mean and what the platforms actually document. It does not cover how to test what AI tools say about your practice — that is [checking AI answers about your practice](/blog/checking-dental-practice-ai-answers/) — or how to interpret AI referral data, covered in [measuring AI referrals and citations](/blog/measuring-ai-referrals-and-citations/).

## The terms in plain language

Much of the confusion comes from vocabulary. Some terms are product names with official documentation; others are industry labels with no agreed definition.

| Term | What it usually refers to | Who defines it |
|---|---|---|
| GEO (generative engine optimisation) | Work intended to make content more likely to be used and cited in AI-generated answers | Industry label; no official platform definition found |
| AEO (answer engine optimisation) | Older label for structuring content to answer questions directly, from featured snippets to voice assistants and now AI answers | Industry label |
| AI search | Any search experience that generates a written answer with sources | General term |
| AI Overviews | Google's AI-generated summaries shown on some search results pages, with links | Google product; documented on Search Central |
| AI Mode | Google's conversational AI search experience for exploratory or comparative questions | Google product; documented on Search Central |
| ChatGPT search | OpenAI's search feature within ChatGPT that surfaces websites in answers | OpenAI product; crawler documented by OpenAI |

When a proposal uses GEO or AEO, ask which products it means, what will change on the website or profiles, and how the outcome will be checked. Labels without those answers are hard to evaluate.

## What Google documents about AI Overviews and AI Mode

Google's Search Central page "AI features and your website" is unusually direct. It states that SEO practices remain relevant for AI features, that "there are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary," and that to be eligible as a supporting link a page must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ai-features"]} />

Several other points on that page matter for a practice:

- **Query fan-out.** Google says both features may issue multiple related searches across subtopics and data sources to build a response. A single patient question can therefore draw on several different pages.
- **Variation.** AI Mode and AI Overviews may use different models and techniques, so responses and links vary, and AI Overviews appear only when Google's systems judge them additive, so they often do not trigger.
- **No special files.** Google says you do not need to create new machine-readable files, AI text files or markup, and there is no special Schema.org structured data required.
- **Business Profile.** Among the fundamentals it lists is checking that Business Profile information is up to date — directly relevant to local practices.
- **Structured data.** It should match the visible text on the page.
- **Controls.** Robots.txt rules for Googlebot are the control for crawling; nosnippet, data-nosnippet, max-snippet and noindex limit what is shown.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ai-features"]} />

On measurement, the same page says traffic from AI features is included in overall search traffic in Search Console's Performance report, within the Web search type, and Search Console's help confirms that a click on an external link in an AI Overview or AI Mode counts as an ordinary click. What that data, and analytics referral data, can and cannot prove is the subject of [what AI referral and citation data can establish](/blog/measuring-ai-referrals-and-citations/).

<SourceNote ids={["google-search-console-performance-metrics"]} />

## What OpenAI documents about ChatGPT search

OpenAI publishes an overview of its crawlers. The distinctions are practical for any practice deciding what to allow in robots.txt:

- **OAI-SearchBot** is used to surface websites in ChatGPT's search features. OpenAI says sites opted out of OAI-SearchBot will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers, though they can still appear as navigational links, and recommends allowing it to appear in results.
- **GPTBot** crawls content that may be used to train OpenAI's foundation models. Disallowing it signals content should not be used for training.
- **ChatGPT-User** visits pages when a user's request triggers it. OpenAI says it is not used for automatic crawling, robots.txt rules may not apply to it, and it is not used to determine whether content appears in search.
- The settings are independent, and OpenAI notes that it can take about 24 hours after a robots.txt update for its systems to adjust.

<SourceNote ids={["openai-crawlers"]} />

<Callout title="A common robots.txt mistake" tone="caution">
Some sites block every AI-related user agent at once, often through a hosting or security plugin setting. For a practice that wants to be found in ChatGPT search, blocking OAI-SearchBot works against that goal. Check the live robots.txt and any CDN or firewall rules; Google's AI features page also mentions that crawling must be allowed by CDN or hosting infrastructure, not only robots.txt.
</Callout>

Google's robots.txt introduction is a useful reminder of scope: robots.txt manages crawling and is not a mechanism for keeping a page out of Google. Decisions about which bots to allow should be deliberate and documented, not inherited from a plugin default.

<SourceNote ids={["google-robots-intro"]} />

## How a patient question becomes several searches

Query fan-out explains why thin websites struggle in AI answers. A patient's question is rarely one fact.

<Illustrative title="One patient question, several underlying lookups">
A patient asks an AI search feature: "Which dentists near the medical centre take my plan and have Saturday appointments for a child's first visit?"

To answer, the system might look for: dental practices near that landmark; which of them treat children; which list that insurance plan; which are open on Saturdays; and reviews or descriptions mentioning children's visits.

A practice whose website lists its insurance plans, shows Saturday hours in text, describes its children's appointments on a page that is linked from the home page, and has a Business Profile with matching hours gives the system something to find at each step. A practice whose hours are only in an image and whose insurance list says "call us" gives it nothing.

The question, practice details and lookups are invented to illustrate the mechanism Google describes; they are not a record of how any specific system answered.
</Illustrative>

Google's helpful content guidance underpins the clarity lane: it asks whether content is people-first or made primarily to gain search rankings. Pages written to say "AI-optimised" things — keyword-heavy FAQ blocks, repeated "near me" phrasing — fall on the wrong side of that test. Accurate profile information is reinforced by Google's Business Profile guidelines, which require the name to match real-world branding.

## Claims to question in AI search proposals

| Claim | What the documentation says | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| "You need an llms.txt or AI file to appear in Google's AI features" | Google: no new machine-readable files or AI text files are needed | Which platform requires it, and where is that documented? |
| "Special AI schema will get you into AI Overviews" | Google: no special Schema.org structured data is needed | What existing markup is wrong, and does it match the page? |
| "We will get you ranked first in ChatGPT" | Responses vary; no platform publishes a ranking position for answers | How will results be checked, and on which questions? |
| "Block AI bots to protect your content" | OpenAI: blocking OAI-SearchBot removes the site from ChatGPT search answers | Which bot, for which purpose, and what do we lose? |
| "We can measure your AI share of voice precisely" | The full set of AI answers is not observable | What sample, what dates, and what margin of error? |

The point is not that every vendor claim is wrong, but that each one should be traceable to documentation or to a test the practice can repeat.

## Tradeoffs and common mistakes

- **Treating AI search as a separate project.** It shares its foundations with local SEO; a separate budget for "GEO" that ignores an inaccurate Business Profile has its priorities reversed.
- **Producing large volumes of AI-written pages.** Google's spam policies list using generative AI tools "to generate many pages without adding value for users" as an example of scaled content abuse, whatever tool produced them.
- **Over-blocking.** Blocking all AI agents can remove a practice from AI search answers it would want to appear in.
- **Under-checking.** Because answers vary, a single screenshot proves little. A repeatable sample over time tells you more.
- **Ignoring capacity.** Being recommended by an AI answer for weekend appointments is a problem if the practice has no Saturday availability to offer.
