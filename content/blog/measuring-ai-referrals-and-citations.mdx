<KeyAnswer>AI referral data can establish that identifiable visits arrived from assistants such as ChatGPT, which pages they landed on and what those visitors did next. Search Console can show impressions of your links in Google's AI features. Manual citation checks show what answers said. None of these alone can establish how many patients chose the practice because of AI.</KeyAnswer>

The question this page owns is narrow: what each kind of AI measurement can support, so that a practice reading a report, or an agency writing one, makes only the claims the data allows. How to run the manual answer checks themselves is covered in [checking whether AI answers describe the practice accurately](/blog/checking-dental-practice-ai-answers/).

## Three kinds of evidence, three different questions

| Evidence | Where it comes from | What it can establish | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI referral sessions | GA4 or another analytics tool, from referrer or campaign tags | That a visit came from a labelled AI assistant; the landing page; engagement; inquiries started on the site | Visits that arrived without a referrer or tag; people who read an answer and phoned or walked in without visiting |
| Google AI feature impressions | Search Console Generative AI performance report and Performance report (Web) | How often links to your pages were shown in AI Overviews and AI Mode, by page, country, device and date | What the answer said; whether the practice was recommended; anything about other assistants |
| Observed citations and answers | Your own prompt sample, logged under recorded conditions | What specific answers said, which sources they cited, whether facts were right | How often real patients see those answers; whether a change caused a change in bookings |

The mistake to avoid is letting one column answer another column's question. Impression counts do not say whether the answer was flattering. A screenshot of a good answer does not say how many people saw it.

## How AI assistant visits appear in GA4

### The AI Assistant default channel

GA4's default channel group now includes an **AI Assistant** channel, described as the channel by which users arrive "from sources like ChatGPT, Gemini, Deepseek, Copilot, or Grok". GA4 assigns it when the medium exactly matches "ai-assistant", and sets the medium to "ai-assistant" when the referrer matches Google's list of AI assistants. The same page states that the channel excludes Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode.

<SourceNote ids={["ga4-default-channel-group"]} />

That gives a practice a usable starting report: sessions, engaged sessions and key events (form submissions, booking starts, tracked call clicks) for the AI Assistant channel, broken down by session source and landing page.

### Referrers and campaign tags

Two mechanisms put a visit into that channel or let you find it manually. The first is the referrer: when someone clicks a link inside an assistant's web interface, the browser can pass the assistant's domain (for example chatgpt.com) as the referring site. The second is a campaign tag on the link itself. OpenAI's publisher help documentation describes ChatGPT adding a utm_source=chatgpt.com parameter to referral links. We could not open that help page on 8 November 2026 because it blocked automated access, so treat the parameter as something to confirm in your own landing-page URLs rather than a documented constant. Filter GA4 for a session source containing "chatgpt" and inspect the full page location to see what actually arrives.

Neither mechanism is complete. Links opened from a desktop app, copied into a new tab or passed through privacy tools can arrive with no referrer and no tag, and GA4 will usually record them as Direct. Any count of AI sessions is therefore a floor, not a total.

### Crawler visits are not visitors

Server logs will also show OpenAI's crawlers. OpenAI documents separate user agents: OAI-SearchBot to surface sites in ChatGPT search results, GPTBot for content that may be used in training, and ChatGPT-User for certain actions a user triggers in ChatGPT, which OpenAI says is not used for automatic crawling or to decide search inclusion. These requests show access, not patients, and should never be added to traffic totals.

<SourceNote ids={["openai-crawlers"]} />

## How Google's AI features are reported

Google's AI features are a different case from standalone assistants, because they sit inside Google Search.

Google's documentation says sites appearing in AI Overviews and AI Mode "are included in the overall search traffic in Search Console", reported in the Performance report within the "Web" search type. In GA4, those clicks belong to organic search, which is why the AI Assistant channel excludes them.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ai-features-search", "ga4-default-channel-group"]} />

Since 31 August 2026, Search Console has also offered a **Generative AI performance report** to all sites. It shows impressions, meaning how many times links to the site were shown to a user in AI Overviews or AI Mode, grouped by page, country, date or device, with the same 1,000-row and aggregation limits as the main Performance report. Its data comes from the Web search type, and it excludes Search Labs experiments. The documentation describes impressions; it does not describe click or answer-content data in this report.

<SourceNote ids={["gsc-generative-ai-performance-report", "google-search-console-performance"]} />

Two practical notes follow. First, if the report is missing, Google says that usually means the site has not had enough impressions in those features, or that the site has been excluded using the Search generative AI control in Search Console settings. Check that setting before drawing conclusions; inclusion is the default. Second, the page dimension is assigned to canonical URLs, so a location page with canonical problems may report under a different URL than expected.

<SourceNote ids={["gsc-generative-ai-performance-report", "gsc-search-generative-ai-control"]} />

## From AI session to attended visit

A dental practice cares about AI traffic only as far as it becomes patients. The chain below is the same one any channel needs, and each step needs its own data source.

Definitions for these stages, and for terms such as key event and qualified inquiry, live in the [dental marketing metrics reference](/resources/dental-marketing-metrics/). How to separate inquiries from bookings, visits and revenue across all channels is the subject of [telling leads, bookings, visits and revenue apart](/blog/dental-marketing-roi/).

The step that matters most is the jump from inquiry to booked appointment. If the front desk does not record how a new patient found the practice, or the booking system drops the source, AI sessions can be counted but never valued. That is a tracking and process problem, not an AI problem, and it sits with [dental conversion tracking](/services/dental-conversion-tracking/).

## A worked example of an honest monthly AI report

<Illustrative title="A single-office general practice's AI section in a monthly report">
**Sessions.** GA4 AI Assistant channel: 38 sessions, up from 29 the month before. 31 from chatgpt.com, 4 from Copilot, 3 from Gemini. AI sessions are 1.9% of all sessions, against 1.6% last month.

**Landing pages.** 17 sessions landed on the emergency dentistry page, 9 on the homepage, 6 on the insurance page, 6 elsewhere.

**Inquiries.** 5 key events from AI sessions: 3 booking-form submissions, 2 tracked call clicks. The front desk log matches 2 of the 3 forms to booked appointments; 1 attended so far. Calls cannot be matched to appointments yet.

**Google AI features.** Search Console Generative AI report: 412 impressions for the month, 60% on the emergency page. No click data is claimed from this report.

**Observed answers.** In the 18-prompt sample run on the 3rd, the practice was mentioned in 10 of 36 answers; one answer still lists an old Saturday hour, citing a directory now corrected.

**What we will not claim.** That AI produced new patients beyond the one attended visit we can trace; that the rise from 29 to 38 sessions reflects our work rather than general growth in assistant use.
</Illustrative>

The useful part of the example is the last paragraph. A report that lists what it cannot conclude is more trustworthy, and more useful for budgeting, than one that converts every number into implied revenue.

## Limits of attribution, and how to work with them

- **Dark AI influence is real and unmeasurable from web data alone.** A patient can read an assistant's answer, search the practice name in Google, and book from the Business Profile. Analytics will credit branded search or Maps. Adding "how did you hear about us" to intake, with "AI assistant or chatbot" as an option, gives a rough, self-reported check. Treat it as directional.
- **Small numbers swing.** For many practices AI sessions are a small share of traffic. Report counts and keep rates for when volumes are large enough to be stable over several months.
- **Labels change.** GA4 maintains the AI assistant referrer list and can change it; assistants change how they pass referrers. Note any definition change in the report where it happens.
- **Third-party scores are samples.** Google's AI optimization guide warns that no third-party tool has access to its internal ranking or AI systems. Visibility scores from tools are observations of their own prompt sets, useful for trend if the method is stable, not measurements of what patients see.

<SourceNote ids={["google-ai-optimization-guide"]} />

## Common mistakes

1. **Summing AI sessions, impressions and observed mentions into one "AI visibility" number.** They measure different things on different scales.
2. **Ignoring Direct.** A rise in Direct traffic to deep pages, such as a specific treatment page, alongside rising AI sessions may include untagged AI visits. Note it as a possibility; do not reclassify it.
3. **Counting crawler hits as reach.** OAI-SearchBot and GPTBot requests show access, not audience.
4. **Not checking the Search generative AI setting.** A practice that is excluded will see nothing in the report and nothing in Google's AI features; that is a settings question, not a performance one.
5. **Stopping at the inquiry.** Without booked and attended visits, AI cannot be compared fairly with paid search, organic or referrals.
